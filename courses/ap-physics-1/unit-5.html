Core ideas

Everything you learned about straight-line motion has a rotational twin. Position becomes angle, velocity becomes angular velocity, force becomes torque, and mass becomes rotational inertia. The big idea: a net torque causes angular acceleration, and how much depends on how the mass is spread out around the axis.

UNIT 5.1Rotational kinematics

θ, ω and α: kinematics for things that spin.

Angles are measured in radians: one full turn = 2π rad. Counterclockwise is positive by convention.

Angular velocity ω

How fast the angle changes: ω = Δθ/Δt, in rad/s.

Angular acceleration α

How fast ω changes: α = Δω/Δt, in rad/s².

With constant α, the kinematics equations work exactly like the linear ones, with x → θ, v → ω, a → α:

ω = ω₀ + αt     θ = θ₀ + ω₀t + ½αt²     ω² = ω₀² + 2α(θ − θ₀)
Worked example: A wheel turning at 2 rad/s speeds up at 3 rad/s² for 4 s. Find its final angular velocity and the angle it turns through.
ω = 2 + 3(4) = 14 rad/s. Δθ = 2(4) + ½(3)(4²) = 8 + 24 = 32 rad (about 5.1 revolutions).
Worked example: A fan starts from rest with α = 2 rad/s². How fast is it spinning after 25 rad?
ω² = 0 + 2(2)(25) = 100, so ω = 10 rad/s.

Graphs

Same rules as Unit 1: the slope of θ vs. t is ω, the slope of ω vs. t is α, and the area under ω vs. t is Δθ.

Common mistakeMixing revolutions, degrees and radians. Convert first: 1 rev = 2π rad = 360°. And rpm means revolutions per minute: 60 rpm = 1 rev/s = 2π rad/s.

UNIT 5.2Connecting linear and rotational motion

s = rθ, v = rω, a = rα.

s = rθ     v = rω     at = rα     ac = v²/r = ω²r

Every point on a rigid object has the same θ, ω and α. But a point farther from the axis travels a bigger circle, so its linear distance, speed and tangential acceleration are bigger, in proportion to r.

Worked example: A point 0.3 m from the axis of a wheel spinning at 8 rad/s, speeding up at 2 rad/s². Find its speed, its tangential acceleration and its centripetal acceleration.
v = rω = 0.3 × 8 = 2.4 m/s. at = rα = 0.3 × 2 = 0.6 m/s². ac = ω²r = 64 × 0.3 = 19.2 m/s² toward the axis.

Rolling without slipping

For a wheel that rolls without slipping, the distance the center moves equals the arc length turned: vcm = Rω and acm = Rα. The contact point is momentarily at rest.

Detailed explanationTwo kinds of acceleration

Tangential vs. centripetal

A point on a spinning wheel can have two acceleration components. The tangential one (at = rα) changes its speed; it's zero if ω is constant. The centripetal one (ac = ω²r) changes its direction; it's never zero while the wheel turns.

Why radians?

s = rθ only works when θ is in radians, because a radian is defined as the angle where the arc length equals the radius. That's what makes v = rω and a = rα work too.

Gears and belts

Two gears that mesh (or wheels joined by a belt) have the same speed at the edge: r₁ω₁ = r₂ω₂. The smaller gear spins faster.

UNIT 5.3Torque

The rotational version of force.

τ = r⊥F = rF sin θ

Torque measures how effectively a force makes something rotate about an axis. It depends on three things: how big the force is, how far from the axis it's applied (r), and the angle θ between r and F. Units: N·m. Counterclockwise torques are positive, clockwise negative.

The lever arm r⊥ = r sin θ is the perpendicular distance from the axis to the force's line of action. A force pointed straight at (or away from) the axis has zero lever arm and makes zero torque, however big it is.

Worked example: You push a door 0.8 m from its hinges with 25 N at 30° to the door's surface. Find the torque.
τ = rF sin θ = 0.8 × 25 × sin 30° = 0.8 × 25 × 0.5 = 10 N·m. Pushing perpendicular (90°) would give 20 N·m.
Why door handles are far from the hingesSame force, bigger r, bigger torque. That's also why a longer wrench loosens a stuck bolt more easily.

UNIT 5.4Rotational inertia

The rotational version of mass.

I = Σmr²     I′ = Icm + Md²

Rotational inertia (I) is resistance to changes in rotation. It depends on the mass and how far that mass is from the axis. Because r is squared, mass far from the axis counts much more.

Worked example: A light rod has a 2 kg mass 1 m from the axis and a 3 kg mass 0.5 m from it. Find I.
I = Σmr² = 2(1²) + 3(0.5²) = 2 + 0.75 = 2.75 kg·m².

Common shapes (given on the exam when needed)

Object (axis through center)I
Hoop or thin ringMR²
Solid disk or cylinder½MR²
Solid sphere⅖MR²
Thin rod about its center¹⁄₁₂ML²
Thin rod about one end⅓ML²

A hoop has all its mass at R, so it has the most rotational inertia for its mass and radius. A solid disk has mass spread inward, so less.

Changing the axis: the parallel-axis theorem

If you know I about the center of mass, the rotational inertia about a parallel axis a distance d away is I′ = Icm + Md². It's always larger.

Worked example: A 4 kg solid disk of radius 0.5 m. Find I about its center, then about a parallel axis at its edge.
Icm = ½(4)(0.5²) = 0.5 kg·m². At the edge: I′ = 0.5 + 4(0.5²) = 1.5 kg·m².
Detailed explanationThinking about rotational inertia

Same object, different I

Rotational inertia is about an axis, not just about the object. A rod is much harder to spin about one end (⅓ML²) than about its middle (¹⁄₁₂ML²).

Ranking problems

To rank shapes with the same M and R, look at the coefficient: hoop (1) > disk (½) > sphere (⅖). More mass far from the axis means more I.

Ice skaters and tightrope walkers

A tightrope walker carries a long pole to increase I, so a wobble produces a smaller α and they have time to correct it.

UNIT 5.5Rotational equilibrium

Newton's first law for rotation: Στ = 0.

Στ = 0    and    ΣF = 0

An object is in static equilibrium when it doesn't accelerate and doesn't start rotating. That takes two conditions: the forces balance and the torques balance about any axis you choose. This is Newton's first law in rotational form: with zero net torque, ω stays constant.

Worked example: A 3 kg mass sits 1.2 m left of a seesaw's pivot. Where should a 2 kg mass go to balance it?
Counterclockwise torque = clockwise torque: (3)(10)(1.2) = (2)(10)d → d = 1.8 m right of the pivot.

Choosing the pivot

In equilibrium, Στ = 0 about every point, so choose the point that makes the algebra easiest: put it where an unknown force acts, and that force's torque disappears.

Worked example: A light 5 m beam rests on supports at each end (x = 0 and x = 5 m). A 400 N load sits at x = 1 m and a 600 N load at x = 2.5 m. Find the force from the right-hand support.
Take torques about the left support (its force has no torque there): FR(5) = 400(1) + 600(2.5) = 1900, so FR = 380 N. Then ΣF = 0 gives FL = 1000 − 380 = 620 N.
Common mistakeForgetting the beam's own weight. A uniform beam's weight acts at its center.

UNIT 5.6Newton's second law for rotation

Στ = Iα.

α = Στ / I

A net torque causes an angular acceleration, in the direction of the net torque, inversely proportional to the rotational inertia.

Worked example: A 40 N force is applied tangentially 0.2 m from the axis of a wheel with I = 0.5 kg·m². Find α.
τ = rF = 0.2 × 40 = 8 N·m. α = τ/I = 8/0.5 = 16 rad/s².

Pulleys with mass

Until now pulleys were massless, so the tension was the same on both sides. A real pulley has rotational inertia, so it takes a net torque to speed it up, and the tensions on the two sides differ.

Worked example: A 2 kg mass hangs from a string wrapped around a 4 kg solid-disk pulley (R = 0.2 m). Find the mass's acceleration, the tension, and the pulley's α.
Hanging mass: mg − T = ma. Pulley: TR = Iα = (½MR²)(a/R), so T = ½Ma. Add them: mg = (m + ½M)a → a = 20/(2 + 2) = 5 m/s². T = 2(10 − 5) = 10 N. α = a/R = 5/0.2 = 25 rad/s².
The methodDraw a free-body diagram for each object that moves in a line and an extended diagram for each object that rotates. Write ΣF = ma or Στ = Iα for each, connect them with a = rα, and solve.
Detailed explanationCommon setups

Atwood machine with a massive pulley

For masses m₁ > m₂ over a pulley with rotational inertia I: a = (m₁ − m₂)g / (m₁ + m₂ + I/R²). With a massless pulley the I/R² term disappears and you get the Unit 2 result.

Rod pivoted at one end

A uniform rod of length L held horizontal and released: gravity acts at L/2, so τ = MgL/2 and I = ⅓ML², giving α = 3g/(2L) at the start. The torque shrinks as it swings down.

Sign check

The tension on the side the pulley is turning toward is the larger one; the difference T₁ − T₂ times R is the net torque on the pulley.

Common mistakes

Points students lose most often.

  1. Using degrees or revolutions in v = rω or s = rθ. Convert to radians.
  2. Using r instead of the lever arm when the force isn't perpendicular: τ = rF sin θ.
  3. Thinking a bigger force always gives a bigger torque. A force aimed at the axis gives zero torque.
  4. Forgetting the beam's own weight at its center in equilibrium problems.
  5. Assuming equal tensions on both sides of a pulley that has mass.
  6. Saying every point on a wheel has the same speed. Same ω, different v = rω.
  7. Treating rotational inertia as just mass. Where the mass sits matters: hoop > disk > sphere.
