Core ideas

Momentum describes how hard it is to stop something that's moving: it depends on both mass and velocity. The big idea of this unit: in any interaction, momentum is transferred between objects, but the total momentum of a system with no outside push stays the same.

p = mv      FΔt = mΔv      p = p′

Impulse changes momentum

A force acting for a time gives an impulse FΔt, which equals the change in momentum mΔv.

Collisions conserve momentum

When objects collide, the forces between them are equal and opposite, so the momentum one gains, the other loses.

UNIT 4.1Linear momentum

Mass in motion, with a direction.

p = mv

Momentum (p) is mass times velocity. Units: kg·m/s. It's a vector: it points the same way as the velocity, so in one dimension its sign gives its direction.

Worked example: A 1500 kg car moves at 20 m/s east. A 0.15 kg baseball moves at 40 m/s west. Find each momentum.
Car: p = 1500 × 20 = 30,000 kg·m/s east. Ball: p = 0.15 × (−40) = −6 kg·m/s (6 kg·m/s west).

Momentum of a system

The momentum of a system is the vector sum of the momenta of its parts. Opposite directions partly cancel.

Worked example: A 2 kg cart moves right at 5 m/s and a 3 kg cart moves left at 2 m/s. Find the total momentum.
Right as +: (2)(5) + (3)(−2) = 10 − 6 = +4 kg·m/s (to the right).

Velocity of the center of mass

vcm = Σp / Σm = (m₁v₁ + m₂v₂)/(m₁ + m₂)

For the two carts above, vcm = 4/5 = 0.8 m/s. If no net external force acts on the system, vcm never changes, no matter how the carts collide.

Common mistakeAdding momentum sizes without signs. Two equal carts moving toward each other at the same speed have a total momentum of zero.
Detailed explanationMomentum vs. kinetic energy

Two ways to describe motion

Momentum p = mv is a vector; kinetic energy K = ½mv² is a scalar. Two carts moving toward each other can have zero total momentum but plenty of kinetic energy.

Connecting them

K = p²/(2m). For the same momentum, a lighter object has more kinetic energy. That's why a light bullet and a heavy rifle get equal and opposite momentum when fired, but the bullet gets almost all the energy.

Doubling

Doubling speed doubles momentum but quadruples kinetic energy. Doubling mass doubles both.

Center of mass

vcm is the velocity of the system's average position. Internal forces (collisions, explosions) move mass around inside the system but can't change vcm; only a net external force can.

UNIT 4.2Impulse and change in momentum

A force over time changes momentum.

J = FΔt = Δp = mΔv

Impulse (J) is a force times the time it acts. It equals the change in momentum it causes. Units: N·s, the same as kg·m/s. On your sheet: FΔt = mΔv and Δp = mΔv.

Force–time graphs

When the force changes, the impulse is the area under the force–time graph.

Worked example: A 0.5 kg ball at rest is hit by a force that rises to 4 N and back to 0 over 0.2 s (a triangle). Find its final speed.
Impulse = area = ½(0.2)(4) = 0.4 N·s. Δv = J/m = 0.4/0.5 = 0.8 m/s.

Bouncing vs. stopping

Worked example: A 0.06 kg tennis ball hits a wall at 25 m/s and bounces straight back at 25 m/s. Find the impulse from the wall.
Toward the wall as +: Δp = m(v′ − v) = 0.06(−25 − 25) = −3 kg·m/s, i.e. 3 N·s away from the wall. If it had stopped dead, Δp would be only 1.5 kg·m/s.

Stretching the stopping time

Worked example: The same 0.06 kg ball at 25 m/s is caught and stopped in 0.005 s. What is the average force?
F = Δp/Δt = (0.06 × 25)/0.005 = 300 N.
Detailed explanationWhere impulse comes from, and graph reading

From Newton's second law

ΣF = ma = m(Δv/Δt), so ΣF·Δt = mΔv. Impulse is just Newton's second law multiplied by time. The F in FΔt is the net force (or the average net force if it varies).

Areas under graphs

Rectangle: FΔt. Triangle: ½ × base × height. Trapezoid: average of the two heights × base. Area below the time axis is negative impulse.

Average force

For a force that varies, the average force is the impulse divided by the time: Favg = J/Δt. It's the height of a rectangle with the same area.

Why bouncing hurts more

To reverse direction, the object first has to be stopped (Δp = mv) and then sent back (another mv), so Δp = 2mv. A rubber bullet that bounces off transfers more momentum to the target than a clay one that sticks.

Safety design

Airbags, helmets, crumple zones and bending your knees on landing don't change Δp. They increase Δt, which reduces the force.

UNIT 4.3Conservation of linear momentum

Momentum before = momentum after.

p = p′     (m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = m₁v₁′ + m₂v₂′)

If the net external force on a system is zero (or tiny during a brief collision), the system's total momentum doesn't change. Prime (′) means "after".

Why it works

When two objects collide, object 1 pushes object 2 and object 2 pushes object 1 back with an equal and opposite force (Newton's third law), for the same amount of time. So their impulses are equal and opposite: whatever momentum one gains, the other loses.

Choosing the system

Include every object that pushes on another in the interaction. If you include only one cart, the other cart's push is an external force and momentum isn't conserved for that "system".

Explosions and recoil

Worked example: A 4 kg rifle fires a 0.01 kg bullet at 400 m/s. Find the rifle's recoil velocity.
Total p before = 0. After: 4v + 0.01(400) = 0 → v = −4/4 = −1 m/s (1 m/s backward).
Worked example: A 70 kg astronaut at rest throws a 2 kg wrench at 7 m/s. How fast does she move?
0 = 2(7) + 70v → v = −14/70 = −0.2 m/s, opposite the wrench.
Detailed explanationWhen is momentum conserved?

External vs. internal

Forces between objects inside the system come in third-law pairs and cancel in the total. Only external forces (from outside the system) change total momentum.

Brief collisions

Even with friction or gravity acting, a collision lasts so short a time that their impulse is tiny compared with the huge collision forces. So momentum is conserved "just before" to "just after" the collision. Afterward, friction can slow things down.

Vertical direction

For carts on a track, gravity and the normal force are external, but they cancel. Only horizontal momentum matters.

Component by component

Momentum is a vector, so in two dimensions it's conserved separately in x and in y.

UNIT 4.4Elastic and inelastic collisions

Momentum always; kinetic energy only sometimes.

Momentum is conserved in every collision (with no external force). Kinetic energy is a different story:

TypeMomentumKinetic energyWhat you see
Elasticconservedconservedbounce apart, no deformation (ideal)
Inelasticconservedsome lostbounce apart, some energy goes to heat, sound, deformation
Perfectly inelasticconservedmost possible loststick together, same final velocity
Worked example: A 2 kg cart at 5 m/s hits a 3 kg cart moving at −2 m/s, and they stick. Find the final velocity and the kinetic energy lost.
p = p′: (2)(5) + (3)(−2) = 5v′ → v′ = 4/5 = 0.8 m/s. K before = ½(2)(25) + ½(3)(4) = 31 J. K after = ½(5)(0.64) = 1.6 J. Lost: 29.4 J.

Checking an elastic collision

For a collision to be elastic, K before must equal K after. If a problem gives final velocities, compute both kinetic energies to classify it.

Common mistakeThinking kinetic energy is conserved because momentum is. Kinetic energy is conserved only in elastic collisions. Momentum is conserved in all of them.
Detailed explanationElastic collision patterns

Equal masses, one at rest

In an elastic head-on collision, they swap velocities: the moving one stops and the other leaves with its speed (Newton's cradle).

Heavy hits light

A heavy object hitting a light one at rest barely slows down, and the light one shoots off at almost twice the heavy one's speed.

Light hits heavy

A light object bouncing off a heavy one at rest comes back with nearly the same speed; the heavy one barely moves.

Relative speed

In an elastic head-on collision, the speed at which the objects separate equals the speed at which they approached: v₂′ − v₁′ = v₁ − v₂. This gives a second equation alongside p = p′.

Where the lost energy goes

In inelastic collisions, the "lost" kinetic energy becomes internal energy (temperature), sound and permanent deformation. Total energy is still conserved.

UNIT 4.4Collisions in two dimensions

Conserve each component separately.

Momentum is a vector, so conserve it separately in each direction:

Σpx = Σpx′      Σpy = Σpy′
Worked example: A 1000 kg car going east at 20 m/s collides with a 1500 kg car going north at 10 m/s. They stick. Find their velocity after.
x: 1000(20) = 2500vx → vx = 8 m/s. y: 1500(10) = 2500vy → vy = 6 m/s. Speed = √(8² + 6²) = 10 m/s at tan⁻¹(6/8) ≈ 37° north of east.

Multi-step problems: momentum + energy

Using the right law in each stage.

Many free-response questions chain two stages. The key is knowing which law applies to which stage:

During the collision

Brief, big internal forces: use p = p′. Don't use energy conservation here unless it's elastic.

Before or after the collision

Motion with gravity, springs or friction: use E = E′ or work–energy, or kinematics.

Ballistic pendulum

Worked example: A 0.01 kg bullet at 400 m/s embeds in a 2 kg hanging block. How high does the block swing?
Stage 1 (collision, p = p′): 0.01(400) = 2.01v → v ≈ 1.99 m/s. Stage 2 (swing, E = E′): ½v² = gh → h = v²/(2g) = 1.99²/20 ≈ 0.20 m. Using energy conservation for the collision would be wrong: most of the bullet's kinetic energy becomes heat.

Crash, then slide

Worked example: A 2000 kg truck at 15 m/s hits a 1000 kg car at rest, and they lock together and skid with μ = 0.5. How far do they skid?
Collision: 2000(15) = 3000v → v = 10 m/s. Skid: friction does the work: μmgd = ½mv² → d = v²/(2μg) = 100/10 = 10 m.
Order of attackSplit the story into stages at each collision. List what's conserved in each stage. The output of one stage (a speed) is the input to the next.

Common mistakes

Points students lose most often.

  1. Dropping signs: objects moving opposite ways need opposite signs.
  2. Using energy conservation during an inelastic collision.
  3. Saying momentum is only conserved in elastic collisions.
  4. Using Δp = mv for a bounce instead of 2mv.
  5. Thinking a longer stopping time changes Δp (it changes the force).
  6. Including only one object in the system and then claiming conservation.
  7. Forgetting that the stuck-together mass is m₁ + m₂.
