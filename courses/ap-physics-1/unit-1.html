Kinematics
Motion in 1-D and 2-D

Displacement, velocity and acceleration, the four constant-acceleration equations, free fall, motion graphs, vectors, projectiles and relative motion: explanations, worked examples, detailed explanations, animated models, 41 practice questions and a timed practice exam.

Core ideas

Kinematics describes motion without asking what causes it. Everything in this unit comes down to three quantities and how they change: position (where it is), velocity (how fast and which way the position changes), and acceleration (how fast and which way the velocity changes).

Equations do the math

Four constant-acceleration equations connect v₀, v, a, t and Δx. Pick the one that leaves out the quantity you don't know and don't need.

Graphs do the thinking

Slope of position-time is velocity; slope of velocity-time is acceleration; area under velocity-time is displacement.

SummaryChoose a positive direction, give every vector its sign, and the four equations handle the rest. In two dimensions, do the x and y directions separately; time is the only thing they share.

UNIT 1.1Scalars, vectors and displacement

The vocabulary every other topic builds on.

A scalar has only a size: mass, time, distance, speed. A vector has a size and a direction: displacement, velocity, acceleration, force. In one dimension, direction is just a sign: choose one way as positive, and anything pointing the other way is negative.

Distance (scalar)

Total path length traveled. Always positive. Walking 3 m east then 3 m west: distance = 6 m.

Displacement Δx (vector)

Change in position: Δx = xfinal − xinitial. Same walk: Δx = 0.

Speed (scalar)

Distance ÷ time. How fast, regardless of direction.

Velocity (vector)

Displacement ÷ time. How fast and which way. Can be negative.

Worked example: A runner goes 400 m around a track in 80 s and finishes where she started. Find her average speed and average velocity.
Average speed = distance/time = 400/80 = 5 m/s. Average velocity = displacement/time = 0/80 = 0 m/s, because she ends where she began.
Common mistakeTreating distance and displacement as the same thing. They're equal only for straight-line motion that never reverses.
Detailed explanationWhy the sign convention matters

Signs carry direction

In one dimension, the + or − on a vector is its direction. Once you pick "right is positive," a velocity of −4 m/s means 4 m/s to the left. The sign isn't optional; dropping it is the most common source of wrong answers in kinematics.

Position depends on where you put zero

Position values change if you move the origin, but displacement doesn't. Starting at x = 2 m and ending at x = 7 m gives Δx = +5 m; with the origin moved, x = 12 m to 17 m, still Δx = +5 m. Physics only cares about changes.

Scalars from vectors

Speed is the magnitude (absolute value) of velocity at an instant. Average speed, though, is not the magnitude of average velocity: a round trip has nonzero average speed and zero average velocity.

On the exam

Many conceptual questions are built on this difference: a ball thrown up and caught at the same height has zero displacement, zero average velocity, but nonzero distance and average speed.

UNIT 1.2Velocity and acceleration

Rates of change, and what their signs tell you.

vavg = Δx/Δt      aavg = Δv/Δt

Velocity is the rate of change of position. Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity: how many m/s the velocity changes each second, so its units are m/s².

Speeding up or slowing down?

The sign of acceleration alone doesn't tell you. Compare it with the sign of velocity:

vaThe object is…
++speeding up (moving right, faster)
+−slowing down
−−speeding up (moving left, faster)
−+slowing down

Same signs → speeding up. Opposite signs → slowing down.

Worked example: A car's velocity changes from +20 m/s to +5 m/s in 3.0 s. Find its acceleration.
a = Δv/Δt = (5 − 20)/3.0 = −5.0 m/s². Velocity is positive and acceleration negative, so the car is slowing down.
Common mistake"Negative acceleration means slowing down." Not always: a car moving left (negative) with negative acceleration is speeding up.
Detailed explanationAverage vs. instantaneous, and zero velocity

Instantaneous values

Average velocity uses a whole time interval. Instantaneous velocity is the velocity at one moment, which is the slope of the position–time graph at that point (the slope of the tangent line). The speedometer in a car shows instantaneous speed.

Zero velocity doesn't mean zero acceleration

A ball thrown straight up has v = 0 at the top, but its acceleration is still −10 m/s² (gravity never switches off). If acceleration were zero there, the ball would hang in the air forever.

Constant velocity means zero acceleration

If velocity doesn't change (same speed, same direction), a = 0. A car on cruise control along a straight road has zero acceleration even at 30 m/s.

Units

a = 3 m/s² means the velocity changes by 3 m/s every second: 0, 3, 6, 9 m/s after 0, 1, 2, 3 s.

UNIT 1.2The four constant-acceleration equations

The core toolkit of the unit.

When acceleration is constant, five quantities describe the motion: v₀ (starting velocity), v (final velocity), a, t, and Δx. Each equation leaves one of them out. These are the lines on your class equation sheet:

Equation (sheet)Leaves outUse when the problem doesn't mention…
v = v₀ + atΔxdistance
Δx = ½(v + v₀)taacceleration
Δx = v₀t + ½at²vfinal velocity
v² = v₀² + 2aΔxttime

The method

  1. Pick a positive direction.
  2. List v₀, v, a, t, Δx with signs; mark the unknown and the one you don't care about.
  3. Use the equation that leaves out the one you don't care about.
Worked example: A car starts from rest and accelerates at 3.0 m/s² for 5.0 s. How fast is it going, and how far does it travel?
v₀ = 0, a = 3, t = 5. Speed: v = v₀ + at = 0 + 3(5) = 15 m/s. Distance: Δx = v₀t + ½at² = 0 + ½(3)(25) = 37.5 m.
Worked example: A car moving at 20 m/s brakes at 5.0 m/s². How long and how far does it take to stop?
Take forward as +: v₀ = 20, v = 0, a = −5. Time: 0 = 20 − 5t → t = 4.0 s. Distance (no time needed): 0 = 20² + 2(−5)Δx → Δx = 40 m.
Detailed explanationWhere the equations come from, and when they fail

Derivations

Constant acceleration means velocity changes linearly: v = v₀ + at (the definition of a, rearranged). The average velocity is then halfway between v₀ and v, so Δx = ½(v₀ + v)t. Substituting v = v₀ + at gives Δx = v₀t + ½at². Eliminating t between the first two gives v² = v₀² + 2aΔx.

Only for constant acceleration

If acceleration changes (a car that speeds up then cruises), split the motion into pieces where a is constant and solve each piece separately. The final velocity of one piece is the starting velocity of the next.

Two answers from v²

v² = v₀² + 2aΔx gives v², and the square root has a ± sign. Physics decides which: a ball thrown up passes the same height going up (+) and coming down (−).

Hidden values

"Starts from rest" means v₀ = 0. "Comes to a stop" means v = 0. "Dropped" means v₀ = 0 and a = −g (if up is positive). "At the top" of a vertical throw, v = 0.

UNIT 1.2Free fall

Constant acceleration, provided by gravity.

Near Earth's surface, anything moving only under gravity has a constant acceleration of g ≈ 10 m/s² downward, whether it's moving up, down, or momentarily stopped. Mass doesn't matter (ignoring air resistance).

a = −g = −10 m/s²   (taking up as positive)
Worked example: A rock is dropped from a 45 m cliff. How long does it fall, and how fast does it hit?
Down as +: v₀ = 0, a = 10, Δx = 45. Δx = ½at² → 45 = 5t² → t = 3.0 s. v = at = 10(3) = 30 m/s.
Worked example: A ball is thrown straight up at 20 m/s. Find the time to the top, the maximum height, and the total time until it returns to the launch height.
Up as +: at the top v = 0. 0 = 20 − 10t → t = 2.0 s. 0 = 20² − 2(10)Δy → Δy = 20 m. Going up and coming down take equal time, so it returns after 4.0 s, moving at 20 m/s downward.
Worked example: A ball is thrown up at 10 m/s from the top of a 15 m cliff. When does it hit the ground below?
Up as +: Δy = −15 (ends below the start), v₀ = 10, a = −10. −15 = 10t − 5t² → 5t² − 10t − 15 = 0 → t² − 2t − 3 = 0 → (t − 3)(t + 1) = 0 → t = 3.0 s (the negative root is not physical). This is where the quadratic formula on your sheet comes in.
Common mistakeSaying acceleration is zero at the top. Velocity is zero there; acceleration is still −10 m/s².
Detailed explanationSymmetry, signs and the quadratic

Up-and-down symmetry

Without air resistance, the upward trip is a mirror image of the downward trip: same time, and at each height the speed is the same going up and coming down. A ball thrown up at 20 m/s comes back to your hand at 20 m/s.

Pick one sign convention for the whole trip

With up as positive, a = −10 m/s² the entire time, including on the way down. Don't switch signs halfway; the equations handle the turnaround automatically.

When time appears squared

Δx = v₀t + ½at² is quadratic in t. Rearrange to ½at² + v₀t − Δx = 0 and use the quadratic formula. One root is usually negative or describes the wrong part of the motion; choose the one that matches the story.

Heavier objects don't fall faster

Gravity pulls harder on a heavier object, but it also has more inertia, and the two effects cancel exactly. Air resistance is what makes a feather fall slowly; in a vacuum a feather and a hammer land together.

UNIT 1.3Motion graphs

The most-tested skill in Unit 1.

Reading shapes

  • Position–time: straight line = constant velocity; curving upward = positive acceleration; curving downward = negative acceleration; flat = at rest.
  • Velocity–time: flat = constant velocity; straight sloped line = constant acceleration; crossing the axis = turning around.
  • Area below the time axis counts as negative displacement.
Worked example: Using the graph above, find the acceleration during the first 4 s and the total displacement.
Acceleration = slope = (10 − 0)/(4 − 0) = 2.5 m/s². Displacement = area: triangle ½(4)(10) = 20 m plus rectangle (2)(10) = 20 m, total 40 m.
Common mistakeReading the value of a graph when the question asks for its slope (or the reverse). On a position–time graph, the height is position; velocity is the steepness.
Detailed explanationTranslating between graphs

From x–t to v–t

At each time, estimate the slope of the x–t graph and plot it as a v value. A parabola on the x–t graph (constant acceleration) becomes a straight line on the v–t graph. The top of a hill on the x–t graph (where the slope is zero) is where the v–t graph crosses zero.

From v–t to a–t

Slope of v–t becomes the a value. A straight sloped v–t line gives a flat a–t line; a flat v–t line gives a = 0.

Going the other way with areas

To get displacement from a v–t graph, add up areas of rectangles and triangles, counting area below the axis as negative. To get distance traveled, count all areas as positive.

Checking a sketch

Exam questions often ask you to sketch a graph from a description. Check: where the object is at rest (v = 0), where it turns around (v changes sign), and whether it's speeding up or slowing down (compare the signs of v and a).

Curved velocity graphs

If the v–t graph is curved, acceleration is changing, and the four equations no longer apply. Slopes and areas still work, though.

UNIT 1.5Vectors in two dimensions

Components turn one 2-D problem into two 1-D problems.

A vector in two dimensions has an x part and a y part, called its components. For a vector of size A at angle θ above the +x axis:

Ax = A cos θ      Ay = A sin θ

To go back from components to a vector: A = √(Ax² + Ay²) and θ = tan⁻¹(Ay/Ax).

Adding vectors: add all the x components, add all the y components, then rebuild the total.

Worked example: A velocity of 10 m/s points 37° above horizontal. Find its components. (cos 37° ≈ 0.80, sin 37° ≈ 0.60)
vx = 10 × 0.80 = 8.0 m/s; vy = 10 × 0.60 = 6.0 m/s. Check: √(8² + 6²) = 10 ✓.
Detailed explanationGetting components right

Which one gets cosine?

Cosine goes with the side next to (adjacent to) the angle. If θ is measured from the horizontal, the x component uses cos; if θ is measured from the vertical, the y component uses cos. Always check where the angle is measured from.

Signs

Components pointing left or down are negative. A vector at 150° has a negative x component (cos 150° < 0) and a positive y component.

Quick sanity check

Each component must be smaller than the vector itself. If θ is small (nearly horizontal), the x component is nearly the full size; if θ is near 90°, the y component is.

Useful values

sin 30° = cos 60° = 0.5; cos 30° = sin 60° ≈ 0.87; sin 45° = cos 45° ≈ 0.71. The 3-4-5 triangle (37° and 53°) shows up often because its components come out as clean numbers.

UNIT 1.5Projectile motion

Two independent 1-D motions sharing one clock.

A projectile moves under gravity alone after it's launched. The trick: horizontal and vertical motion are independent.

Horizontal (x)

No acceleration: vx = constant, Δx = vxt.

Vertical (y)

Free fall: a = −10 m/s². Use the four equations with vy.

Time is the link. Usually you find the time from the vertical motion, then use it for the horizontal motion.

Launched horizontally

Worked example: A ball rolls off a 20 m cliff at 12 m/s horizontally. How long is it in the air, how far from the base does it land, and how fast is it moving at impact?
Vertical: vy0 = 0, Δy = 20 m down: 20 = ½(10)t² → t = 2.0 s. Horizontal: Δx = 12 × 2.0 = 24 m. At impact vx = 12, vy = 10 × 2 = 20, so speed = √(12² + 20²) ≈ 23 m/s.

Launched at an angle

Worked example: A ball is kicked at 20 m/s, 30° above level ground. Find the time of flight, range and maximum height.
vx = 20 cos 30° ≈ 17.3 m/s; vy = 20 sin 30° = 10 m/s. Time up = 10/10 = 1 s, so time of flight = 2.0 s. Range = 17.3 × 2.0 ≈ 34.6 m. Max height = vy²/(2g) = 100/20 = 5.0 m.
PatternTwo objects released from the same height, one dropped and one launched horizontally, hit the ground at the same time. Horizontal speed doesn't affect falling time.
Detailed explanationProjectile facts the exam tests

At the top

vy = 0 but vx is unchanged, so the speed at the top is vx, not zero. Acceleration is still 10 m/s² downward everywhere.

Range and angle

On level ground, range = (v₀² sin 2θ)/g, largest at 45°. Complementary angles (30° and 60°) give the same range; the higher angle stays in the air longer and goes higher. The formula only works when launch and landing heights are equal.

Different heights

When launch and landing heights differ, find time from Δy = vy0t − ½gt² (often a quadratic), then range = vxt.

Velocity at any moment

Combine the components: v = √(vx² + vy²), direction tan⁻¹(vy/vx) below or above horizontal.

Air resistance

AP Physics 1 problems ignore it unless stated. With it, the range and height are smaller and the path is no longer a symmetric parabola.

UNIT 1.4Reference frames and relative motion

Motion looks different depending on who's watching.

Every velocity is measured relative to something. A passenger sitting on a train is at rest relative to the train but moving at the train's speed relative to the ground.

vA rel ground = vA rel B + vB rel ground
Worked example: A train moves at 30 m/s. A passenger walks toward the front at 2 m/s. What is the passenger's velocity relative to the ground? What if she walks toward the back?
Forward: 2 + 30 = 32 m/s. Backward: −2 + 30 = 28 m/s (still moving forward relative to the ground).
Worked example: Two cars drive toward each other at 20 m/s and 25 m/s. How fast does the gap between them close?
Relative to car A, car B moves at 25 + 20 = 45 m/s toward it.

Crossing a river

The boat's velocity relative to the shore is the vector sum of its velocity relative to the water and the water's velocity.

Worked example: A boat heads straight across an 80 m river at 4 m/s relative to the water. The river flows at 3 m/s. How long does the crossing take, how far downstream does it land, and what is its speed relative to the shore?
Across: t = 80/4 = 20 s (the current doesn't change this). Downstream: 3 × 20 = 60 m. Speed: √(4² + 3²) = 5 m/s.
Detailed explanationReference frames in more depth

All inertial frames are equally valid

A frame moving at constant velocity is as good as one at rest. Someone on a smoothly moving train who drops a ball sees it fall straight down; someone on the ground sees the same ball follow a parabola. Both are correct descriptions.

Acceleration is the same in all of them

Adding a constant velocity to every velocity doesn't change how velocities change, so acceleration is the same in every inertial frame. That's why g = 10 m/s² for the falling ball in both the train and ground frames.

Subtracting velocities

The velocity of A relative to B is vA − vB (both relative to the ground). Two cars moving the same way at 25 and 20 m/s close at 5 m/s; moving toward each other (+25 and −20), at 45 m/s.

Aiming upstream

To land directly across, the boat must point partly upstream so that its upstream component cancels the current. That leaves less of its speed for crossing, so the trip takes longer.

Detailed explanationCommon Unit 1 experiments and errors

Measuring time accurately

Stopwatches add human reaction time (about 0.2 s), a random error that's large compared with short fall times. Photogates or video analysis reduce it. Longer drops make the same reaction error a smaller fraction of the time.

Air resistance as a systematic error

Air resistance makes falling objects take longer, so the calculated g comes out smaller than the true value. Using a dense, compact object reduces it.

Ramp experiments

A cart on a ramp accelerates at less than g (roughly g sin θ). Measuring Δx and t at several distances and graphing Δx vs. t² gives a straight line with slope a/2.

Motion sensors

A motion sensor gives position and velocity graphs directly. The slope of its velocity–time graph is the acceleration, and the area under it is the displacement, which you can check against a meter stick.

Common mistakes

Points students lose most often.

  1. Dropping the sign on a velocity or acceleration.
  2. Saying acceleration is zero at the top of a throw.
  3. Using the four equations when acceleration isn't constant.
  4. Mixing x and y components in projectile problems (only time is shared).
  5. Giving the speed at the top of a projectile's path as zero (it's vx).
  6. Reading a graph's value when the question asks for its slope or area.
  7. Forgetting that "dropped" means v₀ = 0 and "stops" means v = 0.
