Core ideas

ENERGYA scalar quantity

Energy has no direction. Kinetic energy is never negative. Energy changes form (gravitational → kinetic → thermal), but the total for a closed system stays constant.

Work transfers energy into or out of a system.

MOMENTUMA vector quantity

Momentum has direction, so use + and − signs. The total momentum of a system is constant when the net external impulse is negligible.

Impulse, a force applied over a time interval, changes momentum.

SummaryWork (force × distance) changes energy. Impulse (force × time) changes momentum.
Detailed explanationWhy physics needs both energy and momentum

Two ways to add up the same force

Every force acts over some distance and over some time. Add up the force over distance and you get work, which changes kinetic energy. Add up the same force over time and you get impulse, which changes momentum. They're the same push, counted two ways.

Take a 10 N force acting on a 2 kg cart at rest for 3 s. Its acceleration is 5 m/s², so after 3 s it moves at 15 m/s and has covered ½(5)(3²) = 22.5 m. Impulse = 10 × 3 = 30 N·s = Δp = 2 × 15. Work = 10 × 22.5 = 225 J = ΔK = ½(2)(15²). Both bookkeeping methods agree.

Why bother having two?

They conserve under different conditions, and each answers a different kind of question.

Momentum is conserved whenever the net external impulse is zero, even if the objects crunch, stick, heat up or explode. That makes it the tool for collisions, where you usually can't know the forces inside the collision.

Mechanical energy (K + U) is conserved only when no friction or other dissipative forces act. But energy doesn't care about direction, so it handles curved paths, ramps and loops without breaking forces into components.

The difference that matters most

Momentum is a vector: two equal and opposite momenta add to zero. Energy is a scalar: two objects moving in opposite directions both have positive kinetic energy, and those can't cancel. So two carts hitting head-on and stopping dead have zero total momentum before and after (conserved), but their kinetic energy went from large to zero. It became thermal energy, sound and bent metal.

ENERGYForms of energy

The four you need for AP Physics 1. Unit for all energy: the joule (J).

Kinetic energy (K)

K = ½mv²

Energy of motion. Speed is squared, so doubling speed means 4× the kinetic energy. Never negative.

Gravitational potential energy (Ug)

ΔUg = mgΔy

Energy stored by height in an object–Earth system. Higher means more. You choose where y = 0; only changes matter.

Spring (elastic) potential energy (Us)

Us = ½kx²

Stored in a stretched or compressed spring. k = spring stiffness (N/m), x = distance from relaxed length. Double the stretch, 4× the energy.

Thermal (internal) energy

Ethermal = FKFΔx

Often called heat. Friction and squishy collisions turn useful energy into thermal energy. It doesn't disappear, but you can't get it back as motion.

TrapPotential energy belongs to a system (object + Earth, or object + spring), not to one object alone. If an FRQ asks you to "define the system," include Earth if you want to use Ug.
Detailed explanationWhere the energy formulas come from

Kinetic energy: why ½mv²?

Push a mass m from rest with a constant net force F over a distance d. From kinematics, v² = 2ad, so d = v²/(2a). The work done is W = Fd = (ma)(v²/2a) = ½mv². Kinetic energy is just "the work it took to get this object up to speed," and that's also how much work it can do while it stops.

The v² is why speed matters so much. A car at 60 mph carries 4× the energy of one at 30 mph, which is why crash damage rises so steeply with speed.

Gravitational potential energy: why mgΔy?

Lift an object at constant speed through height Δy. You must push up with force mg, so you do work mgΔy. That work isn't lost; it's stored in the object–Earth system and comes back as kinetic energy when the object falls.

Only changes in Ug are physical. You can put y = 0 at the floor, the table or the ceiling. The values of U change, but every ΔU and every answer stays the same. Choosing the lowest point in the problem as zero keeps all your U values positive and is usually easiest.

mgΔy only works near Earth's surface, where g is nearly constant. Far from Earth (satellites, planets) a different formula is used, but that's beyond AP Physics 1.

Spring energy: why ½kx²?

A spring's force grows with stretch: F = kx. Graph F against x and you get a straight line through the origin. The work to stretch it is the area under that line, a triangle: ½ × base × height = ½(x)(kx) = ½kx². The ½ appears because the force starts at zero and builds up, so on average you only push with half the final force.

Thermal energy

Thermal energy is the random jiggling of the atoms in an object. When friction acts, organized motion (the whole block moving one way) becomes disorganized motion (atoms vibrating in every direction). The energy still exists, but you can't turn it fully back into motion, so physicists say it was dissipated, not lost.

ENERGYSystems: choosing what's inside

The AP exam asks you to define a system before almost every energy or momentum argument.

A system is whatever objects you decide to analyze together. Everything else is the environment. Forces between objects inside the system are internal; forces from the environment are external.

Closed (isolated) system

No external work is done on it, so its total energy stays constant. Energy only changes form inside it.

Open system

External forces do work on it, so energy flows in or out. The change in the system's energy equals the work done on it by external forces.

W = ΔE   (work done by outside forces = change in the system's energy)

Which kinds of energy can a system have?

  • Kinetic energy: any system with moving objects, even a single object.
  • Potential energy: only a system containing both interacting objects. Ug needs the object and Earth in the system; Us needs the spring in the system.
  • Internal (thermal) energy: any system where friction or deformation happens between objects inside it.

Kinetic energy depends on the reference frame

Speed is measured relative to something, so K is too. A suitcase on a moving train has kinetic energy relative to the ground but none relative to the train. Within one problem, measure every speed from the same reference frame (almost always the ground).

Worked example: A 2.0 kg book falls 3.0 m. Describe its energy using two different systems.
System = book only. Earth is outside, so gravity is an external force doing work W = mgh = (2)(10)(3) = +60 J on the book. The book's energy (all kinetic) increases by 60 J.
System = book + Earth. Gravity is internal, so no external work is done. The system's Ug decreases by 60 J and its K increases by 60 J. Total energy is constant.
Both give a speed of √(2·10·3) ≈ 7.7 m/s. The two descriptions are equally correct; you just can't mix them.
Common mistakeCounting gravity twice: including both "work done by gravity" and "change in Ug" in the same equation. Gravity appears as one or the other, depending on whether Earth is in your system.
Detailed explanationHow the AP exam tests systems

What "define the system" means in an answer

Name the objects ("the block–spring–Earth system"), then say whether any external force does work on it. That single sentence tells the grader which equation you're about to write and is often worth a point.

Choosing the most useful system

Choose a system that makes the unknown work disappear. If you don't know the normal force or tension, but it does no work (it's perpendicular to the motion), it doesn't matter whether it's internal or external. If friction acts between two objects you want to track, put both in the system so friction converts kinetic energy into internal energy instead of appearing as unknown external work.

Work done on a system of several objects

For a system of two blocks connected by a string, the tension forces are internal. The string pulls forward on one block and backward on the other, and since both move the same distance, their works cancel. That's why you can often treat connected objects as one system and ignore tension entirely when using energy.

Energy and momentum use the same idea

Momentum works the same way: the change in a system's momentum equals the impulse from external forces. A system can be closed for momentum but open for energy, or the other way around. Example: two carts colliding on a track with friction. During the very short collision, friction's impulse is tiny (momentum is essentially conserved), but over the long slide afterward friction removes a lot of energy.

Typical exam phrasing

"Consider the system consisting of the cart and Earth." "Is the mechanical energy of the system conserved? Justify." A full answer names every external force, states whether each does work, and concludes accordingly.

ENERGYWork

Work is how a force adds or removes energy.

W = ΔE = F∥Δx

F∥ is the part of the force parallel to the motion, and Δx is how far the object moves. If the force is at an angle θ to the motion, F∥ = F cos θ.

Only the part of the force that points along the motion does work.

Positive work

Force points with the motion (θ < 90°). Object gains energy.
Ex: pushing a cart forward.

Zero work

Force is perpendicular to the motion (θ = 90°), or nothing moves.
Ex: normal force on a sliding box. Holding a heavy bag still.

Negative work

Force points against the motion (θ > 90°). Object loses energy.
Ex: friction, brakes, gravity on a ball going up.

Work from a graph

On a force vs. position graph, the area under the line is the work. Area below the axis counts as negative.

Worked example: A sled is pulled 10 m with a rope force of 50 N at 60° above the horizontal. How much work does the rope do?
W = F·d·cos θ = (50)(10)(cos 60°) = (50)(10)(0.5) = 250 J. Only the horizontal part of the pull (25 N) does work, because the sled moves horizontally.
Detailed explanationUnderstanding work fully

Why only the parallel part counts

Break any force into a part along the motion and a part perpendicular to it. The parallel part speeds the object up or slows it down. The perpendicular part only changes direction or presses on something; it can't change speed. Since kinetic energy depends only on speed, only the parallel part transfers energy. That's what F·d·cos θ picks out: F cos θ is the parallel part.

"Work on" vs. "work by"

Always be clear which object is doing work on which. When you lower a box slowly, gravity does positive work on the box (force down, motion down), and your hands do negative work (force up, motion down). The net work is zero, so the box's speed doesn't change.

Holding a heavy bag: why is the work zero?

If the bag doesn't move, d = 0, so you do zero work on the bag. Your arms still get tired because your muscle fibers constantly contract and relax internally, burning chemical energy into heat. That's work inside your body, not work on the bag.

Work done by gravity doesn't depend on the path

Slide a box down a long gentle ramp or a short steep one from the same height: gravity does the same work, mgh, in both cases. On the steep ramp, the parallel part of gravity is bigger but the distance is shorter, and the two effects cancel exactly. Forces like this are called conservative, and they're the ones that get a potential energy. Friction is non-conservative: a longer path means more negative work.

Variable forces

When the force changes along the way (a spring, or a force graph), F·d doesn't work directly. Instead, the work is the area under the force-vs-position graph. Area above the axis is positive work; area below is negative.

ENERGYThe work-energy theorem

W = ΔE   →   Wnet = ΔK = ½mv'² − ½mv²

A prime (′) means "after": v is the speed before, v′ the speed after.

The net work (all forces added up) equals the change in kinetic energy. Net work positive means it speeds up. Negative means it slows down. Zero means constant speed.

The braking-distance idea (proportional reasoning)

A car braking with the same friction force f: friction does work −f·d, which must remove all of ½mv². So d ∝ v².

Detailed explanationUsing the work-energy theorem well

Where it comes from

Newton's second law says Fnet = ma. Multiply both sides by distance d and use vf² = vi² + 2ad: Fnet·d = m·a·d = ½m(vf² − vi²). So the work-energy theorem is Newton's second law, rewritten in a form that skips time.

"Net work" means every force

You can find Wnet two ways. Add up the work of every individual force (Wgravity + Wfriction + Wapplied + …), or find the net force first and multiply by the displacement. Both give the same answer. A common mistake is to use just one force's work and call it ΔK.

Worked example with several forces

A 5 kg box starts at rest. You push it 4 m across a floor with 30 N horizontally; friction is 10 N.

Wpush = (30)(4) = +120 J. Wfriction = (10)(4)(−1) = −40 J. Wgravity = Wnormal = 0 (both perpendicular). Wnet = 80 J = ½(5)v² → v² = 32 → v ≈ 5.7 m/s.

When to reach for it

Use the work-energy theorem when a problem gives you distances and asks about speeds (or the reverse) and doesn't mention time. If you'd otherwise need a kinematics equation without t, this is usually the faster route.

Proportional reasoning, step by step

For the braking car: friction force f is the same at any speed, so −f·d = 0 − ½mv² gives d = mv²/(2f). Everything except v is constant, so d ∝ v². Write the ratio: d2/d1 = (v2/v1)². This "write the formula, cross out what's constant, compare the ratio" method works for any proportional reasoning question on the exam.

ENERGYConservation of energy

Energy can change forms but is never created or destroyed.

E = E'   (energy before = energy after)

Written out: K + Ug + Us + Win = K' + Ug' + Us' + Ethermal. List every kind of energy the system has before and after, then set the totals equal.

Simplest case, no friction and no outside pushes: Ki + Ui = Kf + Uf. Mechanical energy (K + U) stays constant.

With friction: some mechanical energy turns into thermal energy, so K + U goes down. The total energy (including thermal) is still conserved.

Energy bar charts (LOL diagrams)

These appear often on the exam. Draw a bar for each type of energy at the start, a bar for work done by outside forces, then bars at the end. Total before = total after.

Example: a box slides down a ramp with friction from rest. Start: all Ug (4 blocks, K = 0). End: 3 blocks of K + 1 block of thermal. 4 = 3 + 1. ✔

Worked example: A 50 kg skater starts from rest at the top of a 20 m frictionless hill. How fast is she going at the bottom?
Ui = Kf → mgh = ½mv² → mass cancels → v = √(2gh) = √(2·10·20) = √400 = 20 m/s. Any mass gives the same speed.
Detailed explanationSystems, conservative forces and energy accounting

Choosing a system changes the bookkeeping, not the answer

Take a ball falling from height h. There are two valid ways to describe it:

System = ball only. Earth is outside the system, so gravity is an external force that does work mgh on the ball. There's no potential energy, because the ball alone can't store Ug. W = ΔK → mgh = ½mv².

System = ball + Earth. Gravity is internal. Nothing external does work, so the total energy stays constant: Ug becomes K. mgh = ½mv².

Same result. The mistake is doing both: counting gravity's work and also its potential energy, which double-counts. Pick one approach and stick with it.

Open vs. closed systems

A system is closed (isolated) if no external forces do work on it. Then its total energy is constant. If something outside pushes or pulls through a distance, energy flows in or out as work, and the system's energy changes by exactly that amount.

Conservative vs. non-conservative forces

Gravity and springs are conservative: the energy they take away can come back in full, and the work they do depends only on start and end positions. That's what lets us define a potential energy for them.

Friction, air resistance and the forces in sticky collisions are non-conservative: they convert mechanical energy into thermal energy that doesn't come back as motion. For kinetic friction on a flat surface, the thermal energy produced is ΔEth = fk·d, where d is the sliding distance.

Full example with friction

A 2 kg block starts at rest at the top of a ramp 3 m high and reaches the bottom at 6 m/s. How much energy became thermal?

Initial: U = (2)(10)(3) = 60 J, K = 0. Final: U = 0, K = ½(2)(36) = 36 J. Thermal energy = 60 − 36 = 24 J. Energy is fully accounted for; 24 J just ended up as heat in the block and ramp.

Reading energy bar charts on the exam

Make the bars proportional, mark zero clearly, and include a bar for any work done by external forces. If the system's total changes, an external force must account for it. The exam often asks you to draw bars and then write the matching equation.

ENERGYPendulums & roller coasters

  • At the highest points: it stops for an instant. All energy is Ug.
  • At the bottom: fastest. All energy is K (if you set U = 0 there).
  • In between: a mix. The total stays the same with no air resistance.
  • Speed at bottom: v = √(2gh), where h is how far it dropped vertically (not the string length).
TrapThe tension in the string does zero work because it's always perpendicular to the motion. Only gravity changes the pendulum's speed. Same for the normal force on a roller coaster track.
Roller coastersWithout friction, speed at any point depends only on how far below the starting height you are, not the shape of the track. Two paths that drop the same height give the same final speed (though one may take longer).
Detailed explanationPendulums, loops and finding the height

Finding h from the swing angle

A pendulum of length L pulled back to angle θ from vertical rises above its lowest point by h = L − L cos θ = L(1 − cos θ). The bob is still L from the pivot, but its vertical drop below the pivot is now only L cos θ.

Example: L = 2.0 m, θ = 60°: h = 2.0(1 − 0.5) = 1.0 m. Speed at the bottom = √(2gh) = √20 ≈ 4.5 m/s.

Why tension does no work

The bob always moves along a circle, so its velocity is always tangent to the circle. The string always pulls toward the center, perpendicular to that motion. cos 90° = 0, so tension's work is zero at every instant. That's why only gravity matters for the energy, even though tension is a big force.

Tension is largest at the bottom

At the lowest point the bob is moving fastest along a curve, so it needs the largest net force toward the center (ac = v²/L). Tension must hold up the weight and provide that centripetal force: T = mg + mv²/L. This combines Unit 2 circular motion with energy. Find v with energy first, then use it in the force equation.

Loop-the-loop

For a cart to just make it over the top of a loop of radius r, gravity alone must supply the centripetal force there: mg = mv²/r, so vtop = √(gr). Then use energy to find the starting height: mgh = mg(2r) + ½m(gr) → h = 2.5r. Start any lower and the cart leaves the track before the top.

Why the path shape doesn't matter for speed

Without friction, every bump and dip is just converting between U and K. At any point, ½mv² = mg(drop from start). So speed depends only on how far below the start you are. The shape still affects time: a steeper first drop gets you going sooner, so it can arrive first even at the same final speed.

ENERGYEnergy graphs

Translating between graphs, bar charts and equations is the most heavily weighted skill on the free-response section.

K and U against height or position

For a ball falling freely, Ug = mgh is a straight line against height, and K = mg(h0 − h) is a straight line going the other way. Their sum is a flat line: total energy is constant.

K against time is not a straight line

A dropped ball's speed grows steadily with time (v = gt), so K = ½m(gt)² grows with t². Against time, K curves upward like a parabola, even though against height it's a straight line. Always check the horizontal axis.

Reading a potential energy curve

The dashed line is the system's total energy E. At any position, the gap between E and the U curve is the kinetic energy: K = E − U.

  • The object moves fastest where U is lowest (point C here, then A).
  • It can only be where U ≤ E. Points where U = E (like D) are turning points: K = 0, and the object stops and reverses.
  • Point B is above the E line, so an object starting between D and B can never get past B.
Worked example: A 0.50 kg mass on a spring (k = 200 N/m) oscillates on a frictionless surface with amplitude 0.10 m. Find its speed when it is 0.06 m from equilibrium.
Total energy = U at the turning point = ½(200)(0.10)² = 1.0 J. At x = 0.06 m: U = ½(200)(0.06)² = 0.36 J, so K = 1.0 − 0.36 = 0.64 J. ½(0.50)v² = 0.64 → v² = 2.56 → v = 1.6 m/s.
Detailed explanationGetting every energy-graph question right

Step 1: read the axes before anything else

The same motion gives a straight line against position and a curve against time. Most graph-matching mistakes come from assuming the axis is the one you expected.

Step 2: find what stays constant

In a closed system with no friction, total energy is a horizontal line on any energy graph. With friction, the total mechanical energy line slopes down, and the missing amount appears as thermal energy.

Step 3: use K = E − U

On a U(x) graph, kinetic energy is the vertical distance from the curve up to the total-energy line. Where that gap is biggest, the object is fastest. Where it's zero, the object is momentarily at rest.

Step 4: connect to forces (enrichment)

The steeper the U(x) curve, the stronger the force, and the force always pushes toward lower U, like a ball rolling downhill on the graph. At the bottom of a valley in U (like point A or C), the force is zero and the object is in stable equilibrium.

Bar charts and graphs together

A common FRQ part gives you a bar chart at one position and asks for the graph, or the reverse. Translate one position at a time: read U and K at that point, then check that K + U matches the total.

ENERGYRoller coaster physics

Roller coasters combine everything in this unit: energy conservation, friction, power and circular motion.

1. Speed depends only on height

On a frictionless track, the car's speed at any point depends only on how far it is below where it started:

½v² = ½v₀² + g(hstart − h)

The shape of the track in between doesn't matter, and neither does the mass. Hills only trade kinetic energy for gravitational potential energy and back again.

2. It can never go higher than it started

A car released from rest can't climb above its starting height, because it would need more energy than it has. If it starts with some speed v₀, it can rise an extra v₀²/(2g) above its start.

3. Loops

Which unit?Loop problems combine two units: the speed comes from energy (Unit 3), and the forces at the top or bottom come from circular motion (Unit 2, Topic 2.9). The AP exam can mix them, so it's worth practicing both steps together.

At the top of a loop of radius R, the car is upside down and needs a net force toward the center of mv²/R. Gravity and the track's normal force both point down there:

N + mg = mv²/R   →   minimum speed: vtop = √(gR)

Combining that with energy gives the minimum release height from rest: h = 2.5R.

4. Feeling heavy and light

Bottom of a valley

N = mg + mv²/r

The seat pushes harder than your weight, so you feel heavier.

Top of a hill

N = mg − mv²/r

The seat pushes less, so you feel lighter. If v² = gr, N = 0 and you feel weightless ("airtime").

5. Friction, brakes and lifts

Friction and brakes transform mechanical energy into thermal energy: ΔEth = (U + K)start − (U + K)end. A chain lift does positive work to raise the car; its power is P = mgh/t at constant speed.

Worked example: A car starts from rest 30 m high and goes over a hill whose top is 18 m high with a radius of curvature of 24 m. Do the riders feel weightless at the top?
Speed at the top: v² = 2g(30 − 18) = 240 m²/s². At the top, N = mg − mv²/r. Here v²/r = 240/24 = 10 m/s² = g, so N = mg − mg = 0. Yes: the riders feel weightless for an instant. Any faster and the car would need restraints (or wheels under the track) to keep it from leaving the rails.
Detailed explanationWhy loops are shaped like teardrops, and other coaster details

Why h = 2.5R

At the top of a circular loop, the slowest the car can go is when the track pushes with zero force and gravity alone bends its path: mg = mv²/R, so v² = gR. Energy from release height h to the top (height 2R): gh = g(2R) + ½(gR). Divide by g: h = 2R + 0.5R = 2.5R. Starting exactly at 2R wouldn't work. The car would need zero speed at the top, but it would actually leave the track partway up the loop.

Why real loops aren't circles

For a circular loop with h = 2.5R, the car reaches the bottom with v² = 5gR, and the normal force there is N = mg + m(5gR)/R = 6mg. Riders would feel 6 g's, which is dangerous. Real coasters use a teardrop shape (a clothoid): a tight radius at the top, where the car is slow, and a wide radius at the bottom, where it's fast. That keeps the forces on riders reasonable.

Normal force does no work

The track always pushes perpendicular to the car's motion, so the normal force never changes the car's speed, even though it can be huge. That's why only gravity (and friction) appear in the energy equation, and only heights matter.

Real coasters lose energy

Friction in the wheels and air resistance mean each hill must be a bit lower than the one before. That's why the first hill (after the chain lift) is the tallest, and why a coaster train gradually slows over the ride. The "missing" mechanical energy shows up as thermal energy in the wheels, rails and air.

Using energy and forces together

Most hard coaster problems are two steps: (1) use energy to find the speed at a point, then (2) use Newton's second law in the center-seeking direction to find the normal force. Energy can't give you forces directly, and forces alone can't easily give you speeds along a curved track.

ENERGYPulling or pushing at an angle

Combines force analysis from Unit 2 with work.

When a force is at an angle, split it into parts:

  • Horizontal part F cos θ: this is what does work as the object slides horizontally.
  • Vertical part F sin θ: this changes the normal force, which changes friction.

Pulling up at an angle

FN = mg − F sin θ

The rope supports part of the weight. Normal force is less than the weight → less friction.

Pushing down at an angle

FN = mg + F sin θ

The push adds to the downward force. Normal force is more than the weight → more friction.

Worked example: A 10 kg box is pulled with 40 N at 30° above horizontal across a floor with μk = 0.2. Find the friction force.
N = mg − F sin θ = 100 − 40(0.5) = 80 N. Friction FKF = μFN = 0.2 × 80 = 16 N. (Using N = mg = 100 N would give an incorrect 20 N.)
Detailed explanationAngled forces and ramps in detail

The full method

  1. Draw a free-body diagram with every force.
  2. Split angled forces into components along and perpendicular to the motion.
  3. Use the perpendicular direction (no acceleration there) to find the normal force.
  4. Find friction: f = μN.
  5. Find each force's work, using only the parts along the motion.

Full example

A 10 kg box is pulled 5.0 m across a floor by a 40 N rope at 30° above horizontal. μk = 0.20. The box starts at rest. Find its final speed.

Vertical: N + 40 sin 30° − 100 = 0 → N = 80 N. Friction = 0.20 × 80 = 16 N.

Wrope = 40 cos 30° × 5.0 = 34.6 × 5.0 ≈ 173 J. Wfriction = −16 × 5.0 = −80 J. Net = 93 J = ½(10)v² → v ≈ 4.3 m/s.

Is pulling at an angle better than pulling flat?

Pulling upward at an angle has a trade-off. You lose some forward force (cos θ < 1), but you reduce the normal force and therefore friction. For a small upward angle, the friction you save can outweigh the forward push you lose. That's why people often pull sleds and wagons with the handle tilted up.

Ramps

On an incline of angle θ, gravity splits into mg sin θ down the slope and mg cos θ into the slope. With no other perpendicular forces, N = mg cos θ, so friction = μmg cos θ.

The work done by gravity sliding a distance d down the ramp is (mg sin θ)(d). But d sin θ is just the height dropped, h, so Wgravity = mgh. That's the path-independence of gravity showing up again.

Check your angle

On ramps, the angle between the weight and the "into the slope" direction equals the ramp angle. If you mix up sin and cos, check an extreme: at θ = 0 (flat), the down-slope part should be zero, so it must be sin θ.

ENERGYPower

Power is how fast energy is transferred. Unit: watt (W) = J/s.

P = ΔE/Δt = W/Δt

Not on the sheet but useful at constant speed: P = F∥v (it comes from W/Δt = F∥Δx/Δt).

Two people lift the same box to the same shelf. They do the same work. The one who does it faster uses more power.

Worked example: A 600 N student runs up stairs 5 m tall in 10 s. What's their average power?
Work against gravity = mgh = 600 × 5 = 3000 J. P = 3000 J / 10 s = 300 W.
TrapDon't mix up W (work, in joules) with W (watts, the unit of power). Read the context.
Detailed explanationPower in more depth

Where P = Fv comes from

Power is work per time: P = W/Δt = (F·d)/Δt. And d/Δt is velocity, so P = F·v (using the part of the force along the velocity). This is especially useful when something moves at constant speed against a resisting force.

Constant speed still needs power

A car cruising at 25 m/s against 600 N of air drag and rolling friction has zero net force and zero ΔK. But the engine must still push forward with 600 N to balance the drag, delivering P = (600)(25) = 15,000 W = 15 kW. All of that becomes thermal energy in the air and tires.

Average vs. instantaneous power

P = W/Δt gives the average over the time interval. P = Fv at a particular moment gives the instantaneous power. When a car accelerates with a constant force, its power output rises as its speed rises, since P = Fv.

Power when speeding up

A 1000 kg car goes from 0 to 20 m/s in 8.0 s on level ground (ignore friction). ΔK = ½(1000)(400) = 200,000 J. Average power = 200,000 / 8.0 = 25,000 W. Doing it in 4 s would take double the power for the same energy.

Units

1 W = 1 J/s = 1 kg·m²/s³. A kilowatt-hour (kWh, on electricity bills) is energy, not power: 1 kWh = 1000 W × 3600 s = 3.6 million J. Horsepower: 1 hp ≈ 746 W.

MOMENTUMMomentum basics

p = mv    (units: kg·m/s)
  • Momentum is a vector. Pick a positive direction (say right = +), then anything moving left gets a negative velocity and negative momentum.
  • A slow, massive object and a fast, light one can have equal momentum.
  • For a system of objects, add up the momenta with their signs.
Worked example: A 2 kg cart moves right at 5 m/s. A 3 kg cart moves left at 2 m/s. Total momentum?
p = (2)(+5) + (3)(−2) = 10 − 6 = +4 kg·m/s (to the right). Omitting the sign gives an incorrect 16.
Detailed explanationMomentum, Newton's laws and the center of mass

Newton's second law, the original version

Newton actually wrote his second law in terms of momentum: Fnet = Δp/Δt. Net force is the rate at which momentum changes. When mass is constant, Δp/Δt = m·Δv/Δt = ma, giving the familiar form. The momentum form is more general and leads straight to impulse.

Momentum and kinetic energy are related

Since p = mv and K = ½mv², you can write K = p²/(2m). Two objects with the same momentum can have very different kinetic energies. The lighter one has more K. A 0.01 kg bullet and a 1 kg block both with p = 5 kg·m/s: the bullet has K = 25/0.02 = 1250 J, the block only 12.5 J. That's why a bullet does so much more damage.

Momentum of a system

The total momentum of a group of objects is the vector sum of their momenta. In one dimension, that's adding with signs. In two dimensions (beyond the AP Physics 1 core), you'd add x and y components separately.

Center of mass

Total momentum = (total mass) × (velocity of the center of mass). So if a system's momentum is conserved, its center of mass keeps moving at constant velocity no matter what happens inside. When a firework explodes in midair, the fragments fly everywhere, but their center of mass keeps following the same arc the unexploded shell would have.

When two skaters at rest push apart, the center of mass stays put: the heavier skater moves slower and the lighter one faster, so (m₁v₁ + m₂v₂) stays at zero.

MOMENTUMImpulse

FΔt = mΔv = Δp

FΔt is the impulse (sometimes written J). Δv = v′ − v, the speed after minus the speed before.

Impulse (symbol J) is a force acting over a time. It equals the change in momentum. Units: N·s, which is the same as kg·m/s.

Impulse from a graph

On a force vs. time graph, the area under the curve is the impulse. (Compare: force vs. position area = work.)

The egg drop / airbag idea

To stop an egg, you need a certain Δp no matter what. Since F·Δt = Δp is fixed:

Egg hits a wall

Stops in a short time → big force → breaks.

Egg hits a sheet

Stops over a long time → small force → survives.

Same impulse both times. Airbags, crumple zones, and bending your knees on landing work the same way.

BouncingAn object that rebounds has a larger Δp than one that stops. From +4 m/s to −4 m/s, Δv = −8 m/s, not 0. Rebounding requires more impulse.
Worked example: A 0.15 kg baseball moving left at 30 m/s is hit by a bat with +6000 N for 0.0020 s. Final velocity? (Right = +)
J = (6000)(0.0020) = +12 N·s. Then 12 = 0.15(vf − (−30)) → vf + 30 = 80 → vf = +50 m/s (to the right).
Detailed explanationImpulse, force graphs and why momentum is conserved

Impulse comes straight from Newton's second law

Start with Fnet = Δp/Δt and multiply both sides by Δt: FnetΔt = Δp. The left side is the impulse. So impulse isn't a new law; it's Newton's second law applied over a stretch of time.

What "average force" means

Real collision forces spike and drop over milliseconds. The average force is the constant force that would give the same impulse in the same time. On a force-time graph, it's the height of a rectangle with the same area as the curve.

Graph-reading method

  1. Split the area under the F–t curve into triangles and rectangles.
  2. Add the areas, treating area below the time axis as negative.
  3. That total is Δp. Divide by mass to get Δv.
  4. Add Δv to the initial velocity (with signs) for the final velocity.

Why momentum is conserved in collisions

When cart A hits cart B, Newton's third law says the force of A on B is equal and opposite to the force of B on A, at every instant. The two forces also act for exactly the same time (contact starts and ends together). So the impulses are equal and opposite: whatever momentum A loses, B gains. The total doesn't change. Conservation of momentum is Newton's third law, added up over time.

Why "negligible external impulse" usually holds

Collisions are short. Friction from the floor on two colliding carts might be a few newtons, while the collision force is hundreds or thousands of newtons, all within a fraction of a second. The tiny external impulse (small force × short time) barely changes the total, so we treat momentum as conserved just before and just after the hit.

Safety design, quantitatively

A 70 kg passenger going 15 m/s must lose 1050 kg·m/s of momentum. Stopped by a dashboard in 0.01 s: F = 105,000 N. Stopped by an airbag in 0.15 s: F = 7,000 N. Same impulse, 15× less force.

MOMENTUMSystems and the center of mass

Why momentum is conserved, and how to choose a system so that it is.

A system's total momentum changes only because of external forces: Δpsystem = FextΔt. Internal forces come in Newton's third-law pairs. During a collision, the push of A on B and the push of B on A are equal, opposite and last the same time, so their impulses cancel inside the system.

The center of mass

vcm = (m₁v₁ + m₂v₂) / (m₁ + m₂) = ptotal / mtotal

The center-of-mass velocity is the total momentum divided by the total mass. If no external impulse acts, the total momentum is constant, so the center of mass moves at a constant velocity no matter what happens inside the system: collisions, explosions, push-offs.

Worked example: A 2.0 kg cart moves at +3.0 m/s toward a 1.0 kg cart moving at −3.0 m/s. What is the velocity of their center of mass, before and after they collide?
vcm = [(2)(3) + (1)(−3)] / 3 = +1.0 m/s. Momentum is conserved in the collision, so vcm is still +1.0 m/s afterward, whether they stick or bounce. If they stick, they both move at exactly vcm.

Choosing the system

Ball falling (ball only)

Gravity from Earth is external. The ball's momentum increases. Not conserved.

Ball + Earth

Gravity is internal. The ball gains downward momentum and Earth gains an equal (tiny) upward momentum. Conserved.

Detailed explanationMomentum systems on the exam

When can you treat momentum as conserved?

Strictly, only when the net external force is zero. In practice the exam also accepts "the external force is small compared to the collision forces and acts for a very short time," so its impulse is negligible. Friction on colliding carts and gravity during a brief horizontal collision both fit that description.

Momentum can be conserved in one direction only

A ball rolling off a table: no horizontal forces act, so horizontal momentum is conserved, while gravity changes the vertical momentum. A person jumping off a boat: horizontal momentum of person + boat is conserved even though gravity and the water's buoyant force act vertically.

Center of mass in explosions

When a firework shell explodes in midair, the fragments fly in all directions, but the center of mass continues on the same parabola, because the explosion forces are internal and gravity still acts on the total mass the same way.

Kinetic energy of the center of mass

When objects stick together, they move at vcm afterward. That is the smallest kinetic energy the system can have while keeping its momentum, which is why perfectly inelastic collisions lose the most kinetic energy. In the center-of-mass frame, the total momentum is zero, so a perfectly inelastic collision brings everything to rest in that frame.

Graphs of momentum

On a graph of each object's momentum against time during a collision, the two curves are mirror images: whatever one gains, the other loses. Their sum is a flat line. On a force–time graph, the two forces are equal and opposite at every instant, so the areas (impulses) are equal and opposite.

Detailed explanationCollisions in depth

Why kinetic energy is lost in inelastic collisions

During contact, objects compress. In a perfectly elastic collision they spring back fully, returning all the stored energy as kinetic energy. In a real collision, some compression is permanent (dents), some energy vibrates the atoms (heat), and some leaves as sound. That energy doesn't return as motion, so Kf < Ki.

Perfectly inelastic loses the most

When objects stick, they move at the center-of-mass velocity afterward. That's the lowest kinetic energy the system can have while still keeping its momentum. Any kinetic energy above that amount gets converted. That's why sticking collisions are called perfectly or completely inelastic.

A shortcut for elastic collisions

In a 1D elastic collision, the relative velocity reverses: v₁ − v₂ (before) = −(v₁′ − v₂′) (after). In words, they separate as fast as they approached. Combine this with momentum conservation and you get two simple linear equations instead of a quadratic energy equation.

Special cases worth knowing:

  • Equal masses, one at rest: the moving one stops; the other leaves with its velocity.
  • Light object hits a very heavy one at rest: the light one bounces back at nearly the same speed (ball off a wall).
  • Heavy object hits a light one at rest: the heavy one barely slows; the light one leaves at nearly twice the heavy one's speed.

Explosions run a collision in reverse

In an explosion or push-off, internal energy (chemical, spring, muscle) becomes kinetic energy, so K increases. Momentum is still conserved. If the system starts at rest, the pieces must leave with equal and opposite momenta: m₁v₁ = −m₂v₂. The lighter piece moves faster.

Ballistic pendulum: two stages

A bullet (m) at speed v embeds in a block (M) hanging from strings.

Stage 1, collision: momentum only. mv = (m + M)V. Kinetic energy is NOT conserved; most of it becomes heat.

Stage 2, swing: energy only. ½(m + M)V² = (m + M)gh. Momentum is NOT conserved here, since gravity and string tension are external forces acting for a long time.

Combine: v = ((m + M)/m)·√(2gh). Knowing which law applies in each stage is the whole problem.

Checking which kind of collision occurred

Compute total K before and after. Equal: elastic. Less: inelastic. More: something added energy (an explosion, or a spring released). If K comes out greater in what's supposed to be an ordinary collision, you've probably made a sign error.

MOMENTUMProblem-solving steps

  1. Define the system and pick a positive direction. Give every velocity and force the right sign.
  2. Choose the approach.
    • Impulse approach: a force acts for a known time, or you're given a force-time graph → FΔt = Δp.
    • Conservation approach: things collide or push apart and outside forces are negligible → total p before = total p after.
  3. Identify the interaction: elastic, inelastic, stick together, explosion, or a single push.
  4. Write signed equations, plug in numbers with signs, and solve.
  5. Check: the sign of your answer tells you direction. Units: kg·m/s = N·s. Does K make sense (same for elastic, less for inelastic, more for explosions)?

Detailed explanationA decision process for choosing a method

Ask these questions in order

  1. Is there a collision, explosion or push-off? Use momentum conservation for the moment of interaction.
  2. Is a force or force-time graph given with a time? Use impulse: FΔt = Δp.
  3. Does the problem involve heights, springs or distances, and ask for speed (or the reverse)? Use energy, or the work-energy theorem if forces like friction or pushes act over a distance.
  4. Does it ask for acceleration, a force at one instant, or time without collision? Go back to Newton's laws and kinematics.

Multi-stage problems

Break the motion into stages wherever the physics changes: before the collision, during it, after it. Each stage may need a different law. Write down which law you're using and why. On FRQs, that justification is often worth points by itself.

Common combined setups

  • Ballistic pendulum: momentum (collision), then energy (swing).
  • Block slides down a ramp and hits another block: energy (slide), then momentum (collision), then maybe energy or friction work (sliding to a stop).
  • Spring launches two carts apart: momentum (velocities are opposite, inversely proportional to mass) plus energy (spring energy = total K afterward).

Common mistakes

  1. Forgetting the v². Double speed = 4× K = 4× stopping distance.
  2. Forgetting negative signs in momentum. Objects moving opposite ways have opposite-sign momentum.
  3. Assuming N = mg when a force is at an angle or the surface is tilted.
  4. Saying energy is "lost." On FRQs, say it was transformed into thermal energy. Energy isn't destroyed.
  5. Using energy conservation across a sticky collision. Kinetic energy is NOT conserved there. Use momentum.
  6. Thinking momentum is always conserved. Only when net external impulse is (nearly) zero. A ball falling under gravity gains momentum.
  7. Using string length instead of height drop for a pendulum.
  8. Forgetting that perpendicular forces do zero work (normal force on a flat floor, tension in a circle, gravity on something moving horizontally).
  9. Rebounds: the magnitude of Δv is vi + vf, not the difference of speeds.
  10. Mixing up area meanings: F-vs-x area = work. F-vs-t area = impulse.

Corrections to common notes

Statements that appear in many class notes, with the precise version.

"An impulse causes a change in momentum: p = Ft"The correct form is Δp = F·Δt. Impulse equals the change in momentum.
"Inelastic collisions: both objects stick together"Sticking together is a perfectly (or completely) inelastic collision. An inelastic collision just means kinetic energy isn't conserved. The objects may still separate.
"During collisions or explosions, momentum is conserved"True only when the net external impulse is negligible (e.g., carts on a smooth track, or the collision is so quick that friction doesn't matter). Exam questions sometimes test this condition.
"No thermal energy is generated in an elastic collision"Correct: there is no net loss of K. Objects may deform during contact, but all kinetic energy is recovered.

How points are earned

  • Derivations start from a fundamental equation on the equation sheet (like K₁ + U₁ = K₂ + U₂) and end with the requested variable in terms of the given symbols. Plugging in numbers without the starting equation usually loses the first point.
  • Justifications need a physics principle by name, applied to this situation, ending in a clear conclusion. "Because of energy" earns nothing; "the system's mechanical energy is conserved because no external force does work on it, so the greater height means greater speed" earns the point.
  • Diagrams and graphs are graded on specific features: arrows starting on the object, correct relative lengths, correct curvature, intercepts and labels.
  • Answers are graded part by part. A wrong number in part (b) can still earn full credit in part (c) if you use it correctly ("consistency" credit). Never leave a part blank.
Detailed explanationWriting a paragraph argument that earns full credit

The structure

1. Claim: answer the question directly in the first sentence. 2. Principle: name the law you're using (conservation of energy, impulse–momentum theorem, Newton's second law). 3. Application: connect the principle to the specific objects and quantities in the problem. 4. Conclusion: restate the claim as following from that reasoning.

Example question

"Cart A and cart B (twice the mass) are pushed from rest by the same constant force over the same distance. Which has more kinetic energy at the end? Which has more momentum?"

Weak answer (1/4)

"They have the same energy because the force is the same, but B has more momentum because it's heavier."

Strong answer (4/4)

"The carts have equal kinetic energy, but cart B has more momentum. By the work–energy theorem, each cart's change in kinetic energy equals the net work done on it, W = Fd. The force and distance are the same for both, so both gain the same kinetic energy. Since K = p²/2m, equal kinetic energy means p = √(2mK) is larger for the cart with greater mass, so cart B ends with more momentum. This also agrees with impulse: B accelerates less, takes longer to cover the distance, and so receives a larger impulse FΔt."

Phrases that lose points

"Energy is lost" (say transformed into thermal energy). "It has more force" (objects don't have force; forces are exerted on them). "Momentum is conserved" without naming the system or saying why external impulse is negligible.

Designing experiments

Experimental design questions follow a predictable pattern. Learn the pattern and these become some of the most reliable points on the exam.

A complete procedure includes

  1. What you change (independent variable) and the range of values, e.g. "release the cart from 5 different heights between 0.2 m and 1.0 m."
  2. What you measure (dependent variable) and the instrument: meter stick, stopwatch, motion sensor, photogate, force sensor, electronic balance.
  3. What you keep the same (controlled variables): same cart, same track, same release method.
  4. Repeated trials at each setting, averaged, to reduce random error.
  5. How you'll analyze it: what you'll graph and what the slope means.

Detailed explanationSources of error and how the exam asks about them

Random vs. systematic error

Random error scatters data above and below the true value (reaction time on a stopwatch, reading a meter stick). Repeated trials and averaging reduce it. Systematic error shifts every measurement the same way (friction you ignored, a sensor that isn't zeroed). Repeating doesn't fix it; changing the method does.

Predicting the direction of an error

The exam often asks whether a hidden effect makes the calculated value too large or too small. Work it through the equation. Example: in the spring experiment above, air resistance makes every measured h a little lower. A lower h gives a smaller slope, and k = 2mg × slope, so the calculated k comes out smaller than the true value.

Why the graph should pass through the origin

If theory says h = (k/2mg)x², zero compression should give zero height. A best-fit line with a nonzero intercept points to a systematic error, like measuring h from the floor instead of from the release point. The slope is still useful even if the intercept is off.

Choosing a good range

Spread the independent variable over a wide range with at least five values. A narrow range makes the slope very sensitive to small measurement errors.

Linearizing data

"What should you graph to get a straight line?" appears on nearly every experimental design question.

Detailed explanationLinearization step by step

1. Start from the physics

Write the equation that connects what you change to what you measure, using energy, momentum or Newton's laws.

2. Solve for the measured quantity

Get the dependent variable alone on one side. Everything that doesn't change between trials (masses, g, k, μ) is a constant.

3. Group the constants

Write it as y = (constants) × (something you control). If the variable appears as x², graph against x²; if it appears as √x, graph against √x; if it appears as 1/x, graph against 1/x.

4. Interpret the slope

Set the group of constants equal to the slope and solve for the unknown. Include units: the slope's units are (y units)/(x units), which is a good check that you set it up correctly.

Swapping axes

Either variable can go on either axis; the slope just becomes the reciprocal. If you graph v² against d instead of d against v², the slope is 2μg. Say clearly which way you graphed it.

FRQ-style practice

Write a complete answer before opening the sample.

1. A block is released from rest at the top of a rough ramp. It slides to the bottom. Describe the energy changes of the block–Earth system and explain why the block's speed at the bottom is less than it would be on a frictionless ramp.
Show sample answer
The system starts with gravitational potential energy and no kinetic energy. As the block slides down, Ug decreases. On a frictionless ramp, all of that would become kinetic energy. On the rough ramp, kinetic friction does negative work on the block over the whole distance, so some of the energy is transformed into thermal energy of the block and ramp. Because less energy ends up as kinetic energy, ½mv² is smaller, so the speed at the bottom is lower.

Scoring notes: naming the energy types, saying friction transforms energy into thermal energy (not "loses" it), and linking smaller K to smaller v.
2. A student says, "When two carts stick together, kinetic energy is conserved because energy is always conserved." Explain what is right and wrong about this claim.
Show sample answer
Total energy is always conserved, so that part is right. But kinetic energy alone is not conserved in a sticky (perfectly inelastic) collision. During the collision, some kinetic energy is transformed into thermal energy, sound, and deformation of the carts. So the total kinetic energy after is less than before, while total momentum stays the same (if external impulse is negligible).
3. A 0.050 kg ball is placed against a spring (k = 200 N/m) compressed 0.10 m on a frictionless horizontal surface. (a) How much energy is stored? (b) How fast does the ball leave the spring? (c) If the compression doubled, how would the launch speed change?
Show sample answer
(a) Us = ½kx² = ½(200)(0.10)² = 1.0 J.
(b) All of it becomes K: 1.0 = ½(0.050)v² → v² = 40 → v ≈ 6.3 m/s.
(c) Doubling x makes Us 4× larger, so K is 4× larger. Since K ∝ v², v becomes √4 = 2× faster (about 12.6 m/s).
4. A 0.020 kg bullet moving at 300 m/s embeds in a 1.98 kg block hanging from a string. How high does the block swing?
Show sample answer
Step 1 – collision (momentum): (0.020)(300) = (2.00)v → v = 3.0 m/s. Energy is NOT conserved here because the bullet sticks.
Step 2 – swing (energy): ½mv² = mgh → h = v²/(2g) = 9/20 = 0.45 m.
Applying energy conservation to step 1 is the most common error.
