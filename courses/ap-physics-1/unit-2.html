Core ideas

Unit 1 described motion. Unit 2 explains it: a force is a push or pull from an interaction between two objects, and the net force on an object decides its acceleration.

ΣF = ma

Every problem, same start

Draw the object, draw every force on it, pick axes, write ΣF = ma for each direction.

Net force, not "the force of motion"

Objects don't need a force to keep moving. A net force changes velocity; zero net force means constant velocity (including rest).

UNIT 2.1Systems and center of mass

What you include changes which forces count.

A system is whatever objects you choose to analyze together. Forces between objects inside the system are internal and cancel in pairs; only external forces (from outside the system) can change the motion of the system as a whole.

The center of mass is the average position of the system's mass. For objects in a line:

xcm = (m₁x₁ + m₂x₂ + …)/(m₁ + m₂ + …)
Worked example: A 2 kg ball sits at x = 0 and a 3 kg ball at x = 4 m. Where is the center of mass?
xcm = (2·0 + 3·4)/(2 + 3) = 12/5 = 2.4 m, closer to the heavier ball.

The center of mass of the system moves as if all the mass were there and all the external forces acted on it. That's why you can treat a car, a person or a whole train as a single dot.

Choosing the systemChoose a system that makes the unknown force external if you want to find it, or internal if you want it to cancel. For two blocks pushed together, the whole pair is the easiest system for finding acceleration; one block alone is needed to find the contact force between them.
Detailed explanationInternal and external forces

Why internal forces cancel

By Newton's third law, every internal force has an equal and opposite partner that also acts inside the system. Adding all forces on the system, each pair sums to zero, leaving only external forces.

Example

Two blocks pushed by a hand: the hand's force is external to the two-block system, but the contact force between the blocks is internal. ΣFext = (m₁ + m₂)a gives the acceleration directly.

Center of mass and symmetry

For a uniform, symmetric object (a meter stick, a ball, a box), the center of mass is at the geometric center. It doesn't have to be inside the object: a ring's center of mass is in the empty middle.

UNIT 2.2Forces and free-body diagrams

The first step of every force problem.

Detailed explanationDrawing diagrams the exam accepts

Rules graders check

Draw arrows starting on the dot and pointing away from it. Label each with a clear symbol. Draw only forces on this object; no velocity or acceleration arrows. Make relative lengths reasonable (if the object is in equilibrium, opposite arrows should match).

Normal force isn't always mg

FN equals mg only on a flat surface with no other vertical forces and no vertical acceleration. Pulling up on a rope at an angle reduces it; pushing down increases it; on an incline it's mg cos θ; in an accelerating elevator it's m(g + a).

Tension in an ideal rope

A massless rope pulls with the same tension along its whole length, and an ideal (massless, frictionless) pulley only changes the direction of the tension.

Components

For a force at an angle, replace it with its components on your axes, and then sum the forces separately in each direction.

UNIT 2.4Newton's first law and equilibrium

Zero net force means constant velocity.

An object's velocity stays constant (including staying at rest) unless a net external force acts on it. Tendency to keep moving the same way is called inertia, and mass measures it.

Equilibrium means ΣF = 0, which means a = 0: the object is at rest or moving at constant velocity.

Worked example: A 50 kg crate slides across the floor at a constant 2 m/s while a worker pushes with 150 N. What is the friction force?
Constant velocity → a = 0 → ΣF = 0. Friction must equal the push: 150 N backward. (The 2 m/s doesn't matter.)
Common mistakeThinking a moving object must have a net force in its direction of motion. A hockey puck sliding on ice keeps moving with no forward force at all.

UNIT 2.5Newton's second law

The main equation of the unit.

ΣF = ma

The net force (vector sum of all forces) equals mass times acceleration, and the acceleration points the same way as the net force.

Method

  1. Draw the free-body diagram.
  2. Pick axes, one along the acceleration if you know its direction.
  3. Write ΣF = ma for each axis: forces in the + direction minus forces in the − direction.
  4. Solve.
Worked example: A 20 kg box on a floor (μ = 0.30) is pulled horizontally with 100 N. Find its acceleration.
FG = mg = 200 N, so FN = 200 N. FKF = μFN = 0.30 × 200 = 60 N. ΣF = 100 − 60 = 40 N. a = 40/20 = 2.0 m/s².

Elevators

The scale reading is the normal force. Taking up as positive: FN − mg = ma, so FN = m(g + a).

Worked example: A 60 kg person stands on a scale in an elevator accelerating upward at 2.0 m/s². What does the scale read? What if it accelerates downward at 2.0 m/s²?
Up: FN = 60(10 + 2) = 720 N. Down: FN = 60(10 − 2) = 480 N. Moving at constant speed either way: 600 N.
Detailed explanationGetting ΣF = ma right every time

Signs

Choose positive in the direction of acceleration (if known). Forces that way are +, forces against it are −. If you get a negative acceleration, the object accelerates the other way.

Two axes, two equations

Perpendicular to the surface, the object usually doesn't accelerate, so ΣF = 0 there; that's how you find FN. Along the surface, ΣF = ma gives the acceleration.

Elevator logic

The scale reads more than mg whenever the acceleration is upward: speeding up going up, or slowing down going down. It reads less when the acceleration is downward. Velocity direction doesn't matter, only acceleration. In free fall (a = −g), the scale reads zero.

Mass vs. weight

Mass (kg) is how much stuff and is the same everywhere. Weight (N) is the gravitational force, mg, and depends on g.

UNIT 2.3Newton's third law

Forces come in pairs that act on different objects.

When object A pushes on object B, B pushes back on A with a force of the same size in the opposite direction. The two forces act on different objects, so they never cancel each other.

Worked example: A truck collides with a small car. Which feels the larger force? Which has the larger acceleration?
The forces are equal in size (third-law pair). The car has much less mass, so by a = F/m its acceleration is much larger.
ForceIts third-law partner
Earth pulls the book down (weight)Book pulls Earth up
Table pushes the book up (normal)Book pushes the table down
Common mistakeCalling weight and normal force a third-law pair. They act on the same object (the book) and are different types of force; they're only equal when nothing else acts vertically and there's no vertical acceleration.

UNIT 2.7Static and kinetic friction

The equation sheet lines FKF = μFN and FSF ≤ μFN.

Kinetic (sliding)

FKF = μFN (μ is the kinetic coefficient). Constant size while sliding, opposite the motion.

Static (not sliding)

FSF ≤ μFN. It matches whatever push is applied, up to a maximum. Push harder than the max and the object starts to slide.

Worked example: A 20 kg crate sits on a floor. μs = 0.40 and μk = 0.30. A worker pushes with 50 N, then 100 N. What happens each time?
FN = 200 N. Maximum static friction = 0.40 × 200 = 80 N. At 50 N, static friction matches the push: friction = 50 N, the crate stays put. At 100 N (> 80 N) it slides; kinetic friction = 0.30 × 200 = 60 N, so a = (100 − 60)/20 = 2.0 m/s².
Common mistakeUsing FSF = μFN for an object that isn't about to slip. Static friction is only equal to μFN at the point of slipping; otherwise it's just as big as needed.
Detailed explanationFriction in more depth

Why static is usually bigger

Surfaces at rest settle into tighter contact, so it takes more force to start sliding than to keep sliding: μs > μk for most pairs of surfaces.

Friction can point forward

When you walk, your foot pushes back on the ground and static friction pushes you forward. A car's tires work the same way. Friction opposes slipping between the surfaces, not motion in general.

Area doesn't matter

In the AP model, friction depends only on μ and FN, not on contact area or speed.

Changing the normal force

Pulling a sled with a rope angled upward reduces FN (FN = mg − F sin θ), which reduces friction. Pushing down at an angle increases it.

UNIT 2.7Inclines

Tilt the axes, split the weight.

On a ramp at angle θ, tilt your axes so x points along the ramp. Then split weight into two parts:

along the ramp: mg sin θ     into the ramp: mg cos θ

Perpendicular to the ramp nothing accelerates, so FN = mg cos θ. With no friction, a = g sin θ.

Worked example: A 10 kg box slides down a frictionless 30° ramp. Find the normal force and acceleration.
FN = mg cos 30° = 100 × 0.866 = 86.6 N. a = g sin 30° = 10 × 0.5 = 5.0 m/s².
Worked example: Same box, but now μk = 0.20. Find the acceleration down the ramp.
Along the ramp: mg sin θ − μmg cos θ = ma → a = g(sin θ − μ cos θ) = 10(0.5 − 0.2 × 0.866) ≈ 3.3 m/s².
Detailed explanationIncline cases

Will it slide?

It stays at rest if mg sin θ ≤ μsmg cos θ, i.e. tan θ ≤ μs. The angle where it just starts to slide satisfies tan θ = μs, a common way to measure μs.

Sliding at constant speed

If it slides down at constant velocity, friction exactly balances mg sin θ, so μk = tan θ.

Pushing up the ramp

Friction now points down the ramp, adding to mg sin θ: F − mg sin θ − μmg cos θ = ma.

Checking limits

θ = 0 (flat): sin θ = 0, FN = mg. θ = 90° (vertical): sin θ = 1, a = g (free fall), FN = 0. If your expression doesn't give these, sin and cos are swapped.

UNIT 2.5Connected objects

System first, then one object.

When objects are tied or pushed together, they share one acceleration. Two steps work every time:

  1. Whole system (internal forces cancel): ΣFexternal = (total mass) × a. This gives a.
  2. One object alone: ΣF = ma for that object. This gives the tension or contact force.

Atwood machine

Worked example: Masses of 3 kg and 5 kg hang over a frictionless pulley. Find the acceleration and the tension.
System: the driving force is the weight difference, (5 − 3)(10) = 20 N, moving 8 kg in total: a = 20/8 = 2.5 m/s². Light mass (accelerates up): T − 30 = 3(2.5) → T = 37.5 N.

Block on a table pulled by a hanging mass

Worked example: A 3 kg block on a frictionless table is tied over a pulley to a 2 kg hanging mass. Find a and T.
Only the hanging weight drives the system: a = (2)(10)/(3 + 2) = 4.0 m/s². Table block: T = 3 × 4 = 12 N (less than the 20 N weight, because the hanging mass is accelerating).

Blocks pushed together

Worked example: A 30 N push moves a 4 kg block that pushes a 2 kg block on a frictionless floor. Find a and the contact force between them.
a = 30/6 = 5.0 m/s². The 2 kg block is pushed only by the contact force: F = 2 × 5 = 10 N.
Detailed explanationWhy the tension isn't the hanging weight

Tension vs. weight

If the hanging mass is accelerating down, the tension must be less than its weight (otherwise the net force would not point down). Only when everything is at rest or moving at constant velocity does T = mg.

Adding friction

For the table setup with friction: a = (mhangg − μmtableg)/(mtable + mhang). If the friction is larger than the hanging weight, nothing moves.

Which block gets the push?

When pushing two blocks, the contact force equals the mass of the pushed (front) block times a. Pushing from the other side changes which block that is, so the contact force changes even though a doesn't.

UNIT 2.8Spring forces

Stretch is proportional to force.

FS = kx

Hooke's law: the force from a spring is proportional to how far it's stretched or compressed (x, measured from its natural length). k is the spring constant in N/m: how stiff the spring is. The spring always pulls or pushes back toward its natural length.

Worked example: A 2.0 kg mass hangs at rest from a spring, stretching it 0.10 m. Find k.
At rest: kx = mg → k(0.10) = 20 → k = 200 N/m.
GraphOn a graph of force vs. stretch, the slope is k. This is the standard lab for measuring a spring constant.
Detailed explanationSprings in combination and in labs

Measuring k

Hang several known masses, measure each stretch, and graph F (= mg) on the y-axis against x. The slope is k. Using several masses and a best-fit line reduces random error.

Where x is measured from

x is the change from the spring's natural (unstretched) length, not the total length.

Springs in Unit 3

The stored energy is Us = ½kx². In Unit 2 you only need the force.

UNIT 2.6Gravitational force

Universal gravitation and ratios.

Fg = Gm₁m₂/r²

Every two masses attract each other. G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg², and r is the distance between their centers. Near Earth's surface this becomes FG = mg, with g = GMEarth/R² ≈ 10 m/s².

Ratio reasoning

ChangeEffect on Fg
Double one mass× 2
Double both masses× 4
Double the distance× ¼
Triple the distance× 1/9
Halve the distance× 4
Worked example: A satellite feels a 900 N gravitational force at the surface. What force would it feel at 3 times the distance from Earth's center?
Force ∝ 1/r²: 900/3² = 100 N.
Worked example: On a planet with twice Earth's mass and twice its radius, what is g?
g = GM/R² → (2)/(2²) = ½ of Earth's: 5 m/s².
Detailed explanationGravity in more depth

Inverse-square law

The force spreads out over a sphere whose area grows as r², so the force drops as 1/r². Only distance from center to center counts; "height above the surface" must be added to the radius.

Weightlessness

Astronauts in orbit still feel gravity (about 90% as strong as on the surface). They feel weightless because they and their spacecraft are falling together, so nothing pushes on them.

Gravitational vs. inertial mass

The mass in F = mg is the same as the mass in F = ma, which is why all objects fall with the same acceleration.

Gravitational field

g is also the gravitational field strength: the force per kilogram, in N/kg (same as m/s²).

UNIT 2.9Circular motion

Speed can be constant while velocity changes.

An object moving in a circle at constant speed is still accelerating, because its direction keeps changing. The acceleration points toward the center:

ac = v²/r      v = 2πr/T

The net force toward the center is ΣF = mv²/r. It's not a new kind of force; it's provided by tension, friction, gravity or the normal force.

SituationWhat provides the centripetal forceEquation
Ball on a string (horizontal)tensionT = mv²/r
Car on a flat curvestatic frictionμmg = mv²/r → vmax = √(μgr)
Satellite in orbitgravityGMm/r² = mv²/r
Top of a vertical loopgravity + normal/tension (both down)FN + mg = mv²/r
Bottom of a vertical loopnormal/tension up minus gravityFN − mg = mv²/r
Worked example: A 1000 kg car rounds a flat curve of radius 50 m at 10 m/s. Find the centripetal acceleration and the friction force needed.
ac = 10²/50 = 2.0 m/s². F = ma = 1000 × 2 = 2000 N of static friction toward the center.
Worked example: What is the slowest speed a car can have at the top of a vertical loop of radius 2.5 m without losing contact?
At the minimum speed FN = 0, so gravity alone provides the center-directed force: mg = mv²/r → v = √(gr) = √(10 × 2.5) = 5.0 m/s.
Common mistakeDrawing a "centrifugal force" pointing outward on a free-body diagram. There's no such force in the AP model; the net force points inward.
Detailed explanationCircular motion in more depth

Why inward?

Without a net force, the object would go straight (first law). To bend its path into a circle, something has to keep pulling it toward the center. Cut the string and it leaves along the tangent.

Period and frequency

T is the time for one revolution; f = 1/T is revolutions per second. Speed v = 2πr/T because one lap is a circumference.

Feeling heavier or lighter

At the bottom of a dip, FN = mg + mv²/r, so you feel heavier. At the top of a hill, FN = mg − mv²/r, so you feel lighter, and at v = √(gr) you lose contact.

Orbits

Setting GMm/r² = mv²/r gives v = √(GM/r): orbital speed doesn't depend on the satellite's mass, and higher orbits are slower.

Common mistakes

Points students lose most often.

  1. Assuming FN = mg on inclines, in elevators or with angled pulls.
  2. Using FSF = μFN when the object isn't about to slip.
  3. Adding "ma" or "centrifugal force" to a free-body diagram.
  4. Thinking moving objects need a net force (constant velocity means ΣF = 0).
  5. Calling weight and normal force a third-law pair.
  6. Setting tension equal to the hanging weight when things are accelerating.
  7. Using height above the surface instead of center-to-center distance in Fg = Gm₁m₂/r².
