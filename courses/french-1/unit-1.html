Bienvenue ! Welcome to French

This course takes you from zero to holding a real conversation in French: greeting people, describing yourself and others, talking about what you do every day, and telling what you did yesterday and what you'll do tomorrow. Three things that make French different

Every noun is masculine or feminine. Verbs change their ending for each subject. Many final letters are silent.

1.1Greetings and goodbyes

Saying hello and goodbye the right way for the situation.

French has a formal way and an informal way to say most things. Use the formal one with adults you don't know well, teachers and shopkeepers. Use the informal one with friends, family and people your age.

CultureIn France people usually say Bonjour when they walk into a shop, and friends often greet each other with la bise, a light kiss on each cheek. Skipping Bonjour comes across as rude.
Detailed explanationFormal vs informal greetings

When in doubt, be formal

Bonjour, Au revoir and Comment allez-vous? are never wrong. Salut and Ça va? with a teacher or a stranger sound too casual.

Ça va is a question and an answer

With rising intonation, Ça va ? means "How's it going?". Said flatly, Ça va. means "I'm fine." So a whole conversation can be: — Ça va ? — Ça va, et toi ?

Spaces before punctuation

French puts a space before ? ! : ;. That's why you'll see Ça va ? on these pages. It doesn't change the meaning.

1.3Tu or vous?

Choosing the right word for you.

English has one word for you. French has two.

tu

One person you know well: a friend, a family member, a classmate, a child, a pet.

vous

(1) One person you should be polite to: a teacher, a stranger, an older adult. (2) Any group of two or more people, formal or not.

Tu es fatigué, Max ?Are you tired, Max? (a friend)
Vous êtes prêts, les amis ?Are you ready, guys? (a group of friends: vous because it's plural)
Vous êtes Madame Martin ?Are you Mrs. Martin? (an adult you don't know)
Common mistakeUsing tu for a group. Talking to two friends at once, you must say vous.

1.6Silent letters, liaison and elision

Why French sounds the way it does.

Most final consonants are silent: petit, Paris, vous, ils parlent (the -ent is silent too). The exceptions are usually C, R, F, L; think "CaReFuL": avec, bonjour, neuf, hôtel. (Most -er endings are an exception to the exception: parler ends in an "ay" sound.)

Elision: dropping a vowel

Short words like je, le, la, de, ne, me, te, se drop their vowel and take an apostrophe before a word starting with a vowel or a silent h.

Liaison: linking words

A normally silent final consonant is pronounced when the next word starts with a vowel sound, and it links to that word. A final s or x sounds like z.

les‿amisthe friends: say lay-za-mee
vous‿êtesyou are: say voo-zet
un petit‿enfanta small child: the t links
Nasal vowelsA vowel followed by n or m in the same syllable is often nasal: air goes through your nose and the n isn't fully pronounced. Listen: bon, vin, blanc, un.
Detailed explanationPronunciation rules that cover most words

Final e

A final unaccented e is silent (table, belle), but it makes the consonant before it pronounced: petit (silent t) vs petite (you hear the t). That's how you often hear the feminine form.

Useful letter combinations

ou = "oo" (vous), oi = "wa" (moi), au / eau = "oh" (beau), ai = "eh" (lait), ch = "sh" (chat), gn = "ny" (montagne), qu = "k" (qui), th = "t" (thé).

The French u and r

For u, say "ee" with your lips rounded like for "oo". The r is made in the back of the throat, close to a gentle gargle.

When not to link

Never make a liaison after et (and): un garçon et‿une fille is wrong; say et / une separately. Some words starting with h (called "h aspiré"), like le héros, block both liaison and elision.

From 20 to 69 the pattern is regular: tens + hyphen + ones, like vingt-deux (22) or quarante-sept (47). The only twist: 21, 31, 41, 51, 61 use "et un" with no hyphen: vingt et un, trente et un.

Using numbers

J'ai seize ans.I'm sixteen. (literally: I have sixteen years)
Il y a trente élèves.There are thirty students.
Mon numéro, c'est le 06 12 45 33 21.My number is 06 12 45 33 21. French phone numbers are read in pairs: zéro six, douze, quarante-cinq…
Common mistakeDon't say je suis quinze for your age. Use avoir: J'ai quinze ans. And keep the word ans.
Detailed explanationNumber details

Pronunciation changes

six and dix end in an s sound alone ("sees", "dees"), are silent before a consonant (six livres, "see"), and sound like z before a vowel (six‿ans, "see-zahn"). huit loses its t before a consonant. neuf ans sounds like "neuv‿ahn".

Un vs une

One is un before a masculine noun and une before a feminine one: un livre, une table. The same goes for 21, 31…: vingt et une filles.

Seventeen to nineteen

They are built like "ten-seven": dix-sept, dix-huit, dix-neuf. This "adding" idea comes back in a big way with 70–99.

1.8Subject pronouns

I, you, he, she, we, they.

SingularPlural
1stje (I)nous (we)
2ndtu (you, informal)vous (you, formal or plural)
3rdil (he / it), elle (she / it), on (one, we, people)ils (they, masculine or mixed), elles (they, all feminine)
  • il / elle also mean it: use il for a masculine thing and elle for a feminine one. La voiture ? Elle est rouge. (The car? It's red.)
  • ils is used for any group with at least one masculine person or noun, even ten girls and one boy. elles is only for all-feminine groups.
  • on is used all the time in speech to mean we: On va au cinéma ? (Are we going to the movies?). It takes the same verb form as il.

1.9Être: to be

The verb you'll use in almost every conversation.

être is the most important irregular verb in French. Learn it by heart, out loud.

je suisI amnous sommeswe are
tu esyou arevous êtesyou are
il / elle / on esthe / she / one isils / elles sontthey are

What you can say with être

Je suis de Chicago.I'm from Chicago. (être de + city)
Nous sommes en classe.We're in class.
Tu es sympa !You're nice!
Elles sont canadiennes.They're Canadian (all women).

C'est vs il est

Use c'est (it is / that is / he is / she is) before a name or a word with an article: C'est Marc., C'est un ami., C'est une bonne idée.. Use il est / elle est before an adjective alone or a job with no article: Il est grand., Elle est professeur.

Detailed explanationÊtre in detail

Nationalities and jobs

Nationalities are lowercase in French when they're adjectives: Il est français. They take capital letters only as nouns: un Français (a Frenchman). After être, jobs usually have no article: Je suis étudiant (not "un étudiant").

Where are you from?

Tu es d'où ? or formally D'où êtes-vous ? Answer with Je suis de…; de becomes d' before a vowel: Je suis d'Atlanta.

C'est with plurals

For plurals, c'est becomes ce sont: Ce sont mes amis. In everyday speech many people still say c'est.

Sounds

Être

Common mistakes

Points students lose most often.

  1. Saying Salut or Ça va ? to a teacher or stranger. Use Bonjour and Comment allez-vous ?
  2. Using tu for a group of people. Any group is vous.
  3. Age with être: Je suis 15 is wrong. Say J'ai quinze ans.
  4. Forgetting elision: je aime, le ami. Write j'aime, l'ami.
  5. Pronouncing final letters: Paris has no s sound, ils parlent sounds like il parle.
  6. Mixing up il est and c'est: C'est un ami but Il est sympa.
  7. Forgetting et in 21, 31, 41, 51, 61: vingt et un, not vingt-un.
