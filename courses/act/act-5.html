5.1How to attack a passage

  1. Skim with purpose (3 minutes): read the intro blurb, the first and last sentence of each paragraph, and note the main idea and the author's attitude.
  2. Answer "line" questions first; they're quick and teach you the passage.
  3. Go back to the text for every answer. Find the sentence that proves it.
  4. Paired passages: do Passage A's questions, then B's, then the questions about both.

Passage set 1: literary narrative

Literary Narrative: The Repair Shop

1Every Saturday my grandfather unlocked the shop at seven, though no customer had ever arrived before nine. He said the first two hours belonged to the machines. He would walk the aisles of half-fixed radios and toasters, laying a hand on each one as if checking a fever.

2I was twelve the summer my mother sent me to help him. I had expected to be bored. Instead I learned that a broken thing tells you how it broke, if you are patient enough to listen. A scorch mark meant a short; a rattle meant a loose screw that had traveled; silence, the worst sign, meant the problem was somewhere you couldn't see.

3Customers rarely understood what he charged for. "It was one wire," a man complained once, holding up his mended lamp. My grandfather nodded. "The wire was a dollar," he said. "Knowing which wire was the rest."

4Years later, after the shop closed and the building became a phone store, I stood across the street and watched teenagers carry out bright new devices in plastic bags. None of them, I thought, would ever be laid on a workbench and listened to. I felt something close to grief, and then, unexpectedly, something like gratitude, because I had been taught to listen at all.

Passage set 2: natural science

Natural Science: Why Cities Have Heat Islands

1On a summer afternoon, a downtown street can be several degrees hotter than a park just a few kilometers away. Scientists call this an urban heat island. The main cause is surface: dark asphalt and roofs absorb most of the sunlight that hits them, while grass and trees reflect more of it and cool the air as water evaporates from their leaves.

2Buildings make the problem worse at night. Concrete and brick store heat during the day and release it slowly after sunset, so cities cool off less than the countryside. Tall buildings also block wind that would otherwise carry warm air away.

3The effects are not just uncomfortable. Hotter nights mean more electricity for air conditioning, and heat waves are more dangerous in neighborhoods with few trees. Because these neighborhoods are often lower-income, researchers increasingly describe heat as a question of fairness as well as of weather.

4Some cities are responding with "cool roofs" painted white, new tree planting, and pavement that reflects more light. Early measurements are encouraging: one study found that reflective pavement lowered surface temperatures by about 10°C, though the air just above it changed much less.

Passage set 3: paired passages

Humanities: On Practice (Passage A)

1Musicians often speak of "ten thousand hours" as the price of mastery. But hours alone teach little. A violinist who plays the same piece badly for an hour has practiced playing it badly. What matters is deliberate practice: slow work on the exact passages that cause trouble, with constant attention to mistakes.

Humanities: On Practice (Passage B)

1Deliberate practice is valuable, but it is not the whole story. Many great performers describe long stretches of play: improvising, exploring, playing pieces just for the joy of it. That freedom keeps motivation alive over years. A student who only drills weaknesses may become skilled, but may also stop loving the instrument.
