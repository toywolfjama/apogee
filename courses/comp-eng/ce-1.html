Circuits and transistors

Voltage, current and resistance, how series and parallel circuits behave, and how a transistor turns electricity into logic.

1.1Charge, voltage, current and resistance

Every computer is, at the bottom, a machine that pushes electric charge around. Four quantities describe what is going on in any circuit:

QuantitySymbolUnitWhat it meansWater-pipe picture
ChargeQcoulomb (C)Amount of electricity. One electron is −1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.amount of water
VoltageVvolt (V)Energy per charge: the push that drives current. Always measured between two points.water pressure difference
CurrentIampere (A)Charge flowing past a point per second: I = Q / t.flow rate (litres per second)
ResistanceRohm (Ω)How hard it is for current to flow.a narrow section of pipe

The relationship that ties them together is Ohm's law:

V = I · R     I = V / R     R = V / I

Power is the rate energy is used, in watts (W). Combine it with Ohm's law to get three versions, and pick the one that uses what you know:

P = V · I = I² · R = V² / R
Worked example: A phone charger supplies 5 V and 2 A. What power does it deliver, and how much energy in one hour?
P = VI = 5 × 2 = 10 W. Energy = P × t = 10 W × 3600 s = 36,000 J (= 10 Wh = 0.01 kWh).
Worked example: A 9 V battery is connected to a 450 Ω resistor. Find the current and the power turned into heat.
I = V/R = 9/450 = 0.02 A = 20 mA. P = V²/R = 81/450 = 0.18 W.
Unit prefixesm = milli = 10⁻³ (20 mA = 0.020 A), µ = micro = 10⁻⁶, n = nano = 10⁻⁹, k = kilo = 10³ (4.7 kΩ = 4700 Ω), M = mega = 10⁶, G = giga = 10⁹. A 3 GHz processor ticks 3 × 10⁹ times per second.
Conventional currentEngineers draw current flowing from + to −. Electrons actually drift the other way. Circuit math works the same either way as long as you stay consistent.

1.2Series, parallel and Kirchhoff's laws

Series (one path)

Same current through everything. Voltages add up to the source.

R = R₁ + R₂ + …

Parallel (several paths)

Same voltage across every branch. Currents add up to the total.

1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + …

Two resistors: R = R₁R₂ / (R₁ + R₂)

Two rules work for every circuit, however tangled:

  1. Kirchhoff's current law (KCL): the current flowing into any junction equals the current flowing out. Charge doesn't pile up.
  2. Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL): around any closed loop, the voltage rises and drops add to zero. Energy is conserved.

The voltage divider is everywhere in computer engineering: reading a sensor, setting a reference level, scaling 5 V down to the 3.3 V a microcontroller can take.

Worked example: Three 300 Ω resistors are in parallel across 6 V. Total resistance and total current?
1/R = 3/300, so R = 100 Ω. I = 6/100 = 0.06 A = 60 mA. Each branch carries 20 mA, and 3 × 20 = 60 ✓ (KCL).
Parallel always shrinksAdding a resistor in parallel always lowers the total resistance (you've added another path). The total is always smaller than the smallest branch.
Detailed explanationWhy the parallel formula works

Every branch has the same voltage V, so branch k carries Iₖ = V/Rₖ. By KCL the total current is I = V/R₁ + V/R₂ + … . The equivalent resistance is V/I, so 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + … .

1.3Capacitors, diodes and LEDs

Capacitor

Two plates that store charge: Q = CV, measured in farads (F). It blocks steady current but lets changes through. Charging through a resistor takes about τ = RC seconds to get ~63% of the way. DRAM stores each bit as charge on a tiny capacitor, which leaks, so it must be refreshed thousands of times a second.

Diode

A one-way valve for current. It conducts forward (after about 0.7 V for silicon) and blocks reverse current. Used to protect circuits and to turn AC into DC.

LED

A diode that glows. Each color has a forward voltage (red ≈ 2 V, blue ≈ 3 V). It always needs a series resistor to limit the current, or it burns out.

Semiconductor

Silicon with a few impurity atoms added (doping). n-type has extra electrons, p-type has 'holes'. Where p meets n you get a diode; stack them and you get a transistor.
Worked example: A red LED (forward voltage 2.0 V) should run at 20 mA from a 5 V pin. What resistor?
The resistor takes the leftover voltage: 5 − 2 = 3 V. R = V/I = 3 / 0.020 = 150 Ω. Pick the next standard value up (150 Ω exists) so the current never exceeds 20 mA.
Worked example: A 10 kΩ resistor charges a 100 µF capacitor. What is the time constant?
τ = RC = 10,000 × 0.0001 = 1 s. After 1 s it's at 63%, after about 5τ = 5 s it's essentially full.

1.4Transistors: the switch that makes computing possible

A transistor is a switch with no moving parts, controlled by a voltage. Modern chips use MOSFETs, which have three terminals: the gate (the control), and the source and drain (the two ends of the switch).

TypeTurns ON when gate is…Good at passingDrawn with
NMOS1 (high voltage)a strong 0no bubble
PMOS0 (low voltage)a strong 1a bubble on the gate

CMOS (complementary MOS) pairs them up. PMOS transistors form a pull-up network between the output and the supply VDD; NMOS transistors form a pull-down network between the output and ground. For any input, exactly one network conducts.

  1. Inverter (NOT): one PMOS on top, one NMOS on the bottom. A = 0 turns the PMOS on → Y = 1. A = 1 turns the NMOS on → Y = 0.
  2. NAND: NMOS in series (both must be on to pull Y down), PMOS in parallel. Y = 0 only when A = B = 1.
  3. NOR: NMOS in parallel, PMOS in series. Y = 1 only when A = B = 0.
Why NAND and NOR, not AND and OR?A single CMOS stage naturally inverts. NAND takes 4 transistors; AND is a NAND followed by an inverter, so it takes 6. That's why chip designers think in NANDs and NORs.

Logic levels

A wire doesn't carry a perfect 0 V or 3.3 V. Chips agree on ranges: anything below about 0.8 V reads as 0, anything above about 2 V reads as 1 (for 3.3 V logic), with a forbidden zone in between. This noise margin is why digital circuits are reliable even when the voltages wobble.

Power and Moore's law

A CMOS gate uses almost no power while sitting still; it burns energy when it switches, charging and discharging tiny capacitances. The dynamic power of a chip is

P ≈ α · C · V² · f

where α is the fraction of gates switching each cycle, C the capacitance, V the supply voltage and f the clock frequency. Because power goes with V², lowering the voltage is the strongest lever. Around 2005 chips hit a "power wall": clock speeds stopped climbing past about 4 GHz, and designers switched to putting more cores on each chip.

Moore's law is the observation (Gordon Moore, 1965/1975) that the number of transistors on a chip doubles roughly every two years. A modern processor has tens of billions of transistors.

Detailed explanationDoubling every two years, in numbers

If a chip had 2,300 transistors in 1971 (the Intel 4004) and doubled every 2 years, after 50 years it would have 2,300 × 2²⁵ ≈ 7.7 × 10¹⁰, about 77 billion. Real chips in the early 2020s were in the tens of billions, so the trend held remarkably well for five decades.

Unit summary

Ohm's law

V = IR. Power P = VI = I²R = V²/R.

Series / parallel

Series: R adds, same current. Parallel: 1/R adds, same voltage. KCL: current in = current out. KVL: voltages around a loop sum to 0.

Components

Capacitor stores charge (τ = RC). Diode = one-way valve. LED needs a series resistor: R = (V − V_LED)/I.

Transistors

NMOS on with 1, PMOS on with 0. CMOS: PMOS pull-up + NMOS pull-down. NAND = NMOS series + PMOS parallel. P ≈ αCV²f.
