3.1Sentence boundaries: the punctuation rules

Most grammar questions are about where one sentence ends and the next begins. First decide: is each side of the blank an independent clause (could stand alone as a sentence)?

Between…UseExample
two independent clausesperiod, semicolon, or comma + FANBOYS (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so)It rained; the game went on. / It rained, but the game went on.
independent clause + explanation or listcolon (the part before it must be a full sentence)She needed three things: flour, eggs and time.
extra information in the middlea matching pair: two commas, two dashes, or two parenthesesThe dog — a beagle named Pip — ran off.
dependent + independent clausecomma after the dependent clauseAlthough it rained, the game went on.
subject and its verbNO punctuationThe scientists in the lab found… (not "lab, found")
Colon testCover everything after the colon. If what's left isn't a complete sentence, the colon is wrong. "The recipe calls for: flour and eggs" fails.
ApostrophesPossessive: the scientist's notes (one scientist), the scientists' notes (many). its = belonging to it; it's = it is. their = belonging to them; they're = they are.

3.2Form, structure and sense

Subject-verb agreement

Find the real subject; ignore prepositional phrases. "The list of names is long." Each, every, either, neither and collective nouns like team are singular.

Pronoun agreement

The pronoun must match its noun. "The company announced its plan" (not their). Watch for unclear "it" or "they."

Verb tense

Match the time frame of the surrounding sentences. A finished event in the past: simple past. Past before past: had + verb.

Modifiers

An opening phrase describes whatever comes right after the comma. "Walking to school, the rain soaked me" says the rain walked. Fix: "Walking to school, I got soaked."

3.3Transitions

Read the sentence before the blank and the sentence with the blank. Name the relationship before you look at the choices.

Worked example:

The new bridge cut the commute from 40 minutes to 15. many residents who had moved away began returning to the town.

Faster commute → people return: As a result,. "However" (contrast) and "For example" (illustration) don't fit.

3.4Rhetorical synthesis (the notes questions)

You get a student's bullet-point notes and a goal. Only the goal matters. Underline it, then choose the sentence that does exactly that, using information from the notes.

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

  • The Great Wall of China is not one wall but many walls built over centuries.
  • The longest sections were built during the Ming dynasty (1368–1644).
  • Ming-era walls were made mostly of brick and stone.
  • Earlier walls were often made of packed earth.

The student wants to contrast the materials used in different periods. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

Worked example: Answer
"While earlier sections of the Great Wall were often made of packed earth, Ming-era walls were built mostly of brick and stone."
A sentence about how long the wall is, even if true and well written, fails the goal.
Goal words"Emphasize a similarity," "introduce the study to an audience unfamiliar with it," "present the conclusion," "specify the date." Each word in the goal rules out choices.
