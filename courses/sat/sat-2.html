Reading like a test-maker

Main ideas, evidence from text and data, inferences, words in context, purpose, structure and paired texts.

2.1Central ideas and details

"Which choice best states the main idea?" The right answer covers the whole text, not one sentence, and claims no more than the text supports.

In the 1950s, the biologist Rachel Carson began collecting reports of birds dying after farmers sprayed the pesticide DDT. Rather than relying on a single study, she gathered evidence from scientists, doctors and farmers across the country. Her 1962 book, Silent Spring, combined this evidence into an argument the public could follow, and it helped lead to new limits on pesticide use.

Worked example: Main idea?
Carson persuaded the public by gathering evidence from many sources into one clear argument, which helped change pesticide rules.
Traps: "Carson studied birds" is a detail; "DDT was banned worldwide in 1962" claims more than the text says.
Too narrow, too broad, or offWrong main-idea choices are usually one detail (too narrow), a sweeping claim (too broad), or something the text never says (off). The right answer is boring and accurate.

2.2Command of evidence: text and data

Two kinds: textual (which quotation or finding supports a claim) and quantitative (which choice correctly uses a table or graph). For both, first state the exact claim, then test each choice against it.

Graphs and tables: the right choice must be (1) true according to the data and (2) relevant to the claim in the passage. Many wrong choices are true but answer a different question.

A student claims that increasing light beyond 12 hours per day had little additional effect on growth.

Worked example: Which choice uses the data to support the claim?
"Group C, with 16 hours, averaged 9.6 cm, only slightly taller than group B's 9.4 cm."
"Group B grew more than group A" is true but is about going from 8 to 12 hours, not beyond 12.

2.3Inferences

"Which choice most logically completes the text?" The blank is at the end. Follow the logic chain; the answer must necessarily follow, not just be possible.

Archaeologists found bread wheat seeds at a site dated to 7,000 years ago. Bread wheat does not grow wild in that region; it was first domesticated about 600 kilometers to the east. This suggests that

Worked example: Best completion?
"people at the site traded with, or came from, communities to the east." The seeds had to get there somehow, and wild growth is ruled out.
"wheat was first domesticated at this site" contradicts the text; "bread was the main food" goes beyond it.

2.4Words in context

Cover the choices. Read the sentence and put your own word in the blank. Then pick the choice closest to your word. Watch for second meanings: "novel" can mean new, "qualify" can mean limit, "check" can mean stop.

Although the scientist's early results were promising, she was careful to her conclusions, noting that the sample was small and the study lasted only a month.

Worked example: Your word first
Something like "limit" or "soften." Best choice: qualify. Traps: "publish," "celebrate," "repeat" don't match "careful" + small sample.

2.5Structure, purpose and cross-text

Purpose questions ask why a sentence or text is there. Use verbs: introduces, illustrates, challenges, qualifies, explains a cause. Structure questions ask for the order of moves: "describes a common view, then presents evidence against it."

For decades, scientists assumed that octopuses, being solitary animals, had little reason to recognize individual humans. Yet in one aquarium, an octopus repeatedly sprayed water at a single staff member while ignoring everyone else. Researchers now think octopuses may track individuals far more than expected.

Worked example: Function of the underlined sentence?
It gives an example that challenges the earlier assumption. "Yet" is your signal.

Cross-text questions give Text 1 and Text 2. Summarize each author's position in a few words, then ask: would Author 2 agree, disagree, or add a condition?

Text 1: Short daily practice sessions are the best way to learn an instrument, because the brain consolidates skills during sleep between sessions.

Text 2: Daily practice helps beginners, but advanced musicians often need long, focused sessions to master difficult passages that can't be broken into small pieces.

Worked example: How would the author of Text 2 respond to Text 1?
Partly agree: short sessions work for beginners, but not for every learner. The answer will say Text 1's claim is too general.
